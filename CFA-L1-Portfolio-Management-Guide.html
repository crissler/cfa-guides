Exam game plan

What candidates who passed wish they'd known going in.

What actually gets tested

  • Modules 1–2 (risk and return, CAPM) carry most of the calculations.
  • Modules 3–6 are almost all definitions and "which one is it" questions. They're the cheapest points in the topic if you memorize the lists.
  • Every question has three choices (A, B, C) and there's no penalty for guessing, so never leave one blank.
  • Wrong choices are usually built from a specific mistake: forgetting a square root, using percent instead of decimals, or using σ where β belongs.

Highest-yield items, ranked

  1. Two-asset portfolio variance and the role of correlation
  2. CAPM / SML: required return, over- vs undervalued
  3. Sharpe, Treynor, M², Jensen's alpha: formula and when to use each
  4. Beta from covariance or correlation
  5. Utility, risk aversion and indifference curves
  6. CAL vs CML vs SML vs SCL
  7. Systematic vs nonsystematic risk
  8. Time-weighted vs money-weighted return
  9. Behavioral biases: cognitive vs emotional
  10. IPS objectives and constraints; investor types
Top traps. Using percent in the utility formula (use decimals). Forgetting the square root after computing variance. Using Sharpe (total risk) when the portfolio is only part of a diversified whole (use Treynor). Calling VaR the "maximum" loss (it's a minimum loss in the tail). Treating a risk-free asset's utility as depending on A (it doesn't).
BA II Plus. Money-weighted return = IRR: CF enter CF0, C01, F01 …, then IRR CPT. Geometric mean: multiply the (1 + R) terms, then yx with 1/T, then subtract 1. Set decimals to 4 or more (2nd FORMAT) so rounding doesn't push you to the wrong choice.
Learning module 1

Portfolio risk and return: Part I

Returns, risk, risk aversion, utility, diversification, the efficient frontier and the CAL.

1.1 Return measures

Holding period return
HPR = P1 − P0 + D1P0
Arithmetic mean
R̄ = R1 + R2 + … + RTT
Geometric mean
[(1+R1)(1+R2)…(1+RT)]1/T − 1
Compound growth rate. Always ≤ arithmetic; the gap grows with volatility.
Money-weighted return (IRR)
Σ CFt(1 + IRR)t = 0
Affected by the timing and size of the investor's cash flows.
Time-weighted return
[(1+HPR1)…(1+HPRn)]1/years − 1
Split at every cash flow. Industry standard for judging managers.

RL = Rp + VBVE(Rp − rD)

Also know: gross return (before fees) vs net return (after fees); pre-tax vs after-tax return.

1.2 Historical risk, return and distributions

Long-run ranking (US data, both return and risk)

Small-cap stocks > large-cap stocks > long-term corporate bonds > long-term government bonds > T-bills.

This positive risk–return relationship is the evidence for risk aversion.

Returns aren't normal

  • Negative skew: large losses more common than large gains.
  • Excess kurtosis (fat tails): extreme outcomes happen more often than the normal distribution predicts.
  • Liquidity: illiquid assets have higher trading costs and price impact.

1.3 Risk aversion and utility

InvestorASame expected return, different riskIndifference curves
Risk-averseA > 0Picks the lower-risk choice; needs a premium to take riskSlope up, convex
Risk-neutralA = 0Indifferent; only expected return mattersFlat
Risk-seekingA < 0Picks the riskier choiceSlope down
Utility
U = E(r) − ½ · A · σ²
Use decimals: 10% = 0.10, σ = 20% → σ² = 0.04. Higher U is better. Pick the investment with the highest U for that investor.
Key fact. For a risk-free asset σ = 0, so U = rf for every investor regardless of A. Risk aversion only matters for risky assets. Assume investors are risk-averse unless told otherwise.
Indifference curves
Each curve is one utility level. Investors want the highest curve (up and to the left). Curves for one investor never cross.
U = 4%U = 8%U = 12%Where a curve meets the y-axis, utility = the risk-free return

1.4 Two-asset portfolios

Portfolio return
E(Rp) = w1R1 + w2R2
Portfolio variance
σp² = w1²σ1² + w2²σ2² + 2w1w2ρ1,2σ1σ2
Take the square root for σp.
Covariance and correlation
Cov1,2 = ρ1,2σ1σ2  ⇔  ρ1,2 = Cov1,2σ1σ2
So the last variance term can be written 2w1w2Cov1,2.
ρ = +1: σp is just the weighted average of the σs. No diversification benefit.
ρ < +1: σp is below the weighted average. Lower ρ means more benefit.
ρ = −1: some mix has σp = 0.
Shortcut: if a question gives σp equal to the weighted average of the σs, the answer is ρ = +1.
Outcome tables: you can build the portfolio's return in each scenario directly, then find its variance. No covariance needed.
Minimum-variance frontier (two assets)
Asset A: 6% return, 10% σ. Asset B: 14% return, 25% σ. Move ρ to bend the curve; move the weight to slide your portfolio along it.

1.5 Many assets and diversification

Equal-weighted portfolio of N assets
σp² = σ̄²N + N − 1N · Cov
As N grows, the first term vanishes and σp² → average covariance. Covariance (how assets move together) is the risk you can't diversify away.
How diversification hits a floor
Each stock has σ = 30%. The average correlation sets how low portfolio risk can go.

1.6 Efficient frontier, CAL and the optimal portfolio

Minimum-variance frontier

Lowest risk for each level of return. The leftmost point is the global minimum-variance portfolio.

Efficient frontier

The part above the global minimum-variance portfolio: highest return for each level of risk. Rational investors only choose from here.

Capital allocation line

A straight line from rf through a risky portfolio P. Every point is a mix of T-bills and P. The best CAL is the steepest one, tangent to the frontier.

CAL
E(Rp) = Rf + E(Ri) − Rfσi · σp
Slope = Sharpe ratio of risky portfolio i.
Risk on the CAL
σp = w · σi
You choose σp by choosing w; the risk-free asset adds no risk. w > 1 = borrowing.
Optimal weight in the risky portfolio
w* = E(Ri) − RfA · σi²
The capital allocation line
The tangency portfolio P doesn't depend on risk aversion. Only your position on the line does.
Optimal CALA worse CALEfficient frontierYour indifference curve
Slope (Sharpe ratio)
Weight in P
Position
Two-fund separation. Everyone holds the same two "funds": the risk-free asset and the optimal risky portfolio. The investment decision (which risky portfolio) is the same for all; the financing decision (how much in it) depends on each investor's risk aversion.
Learning module 2

Portfolio risk and return: Part II

CML, systematic risk, beta, CAPM and the SML, and performance measures.

2.1 Capital market line

CML
E(Rp) = Rf + E(Rm) − Rfσm · σp
The CAL whose risky portfolio is the market portfolio. With homogeneous expectations, everyone's optimal CAL is the CML.
  • Left of M: lending portfolios (part T-bills). Right of M: borrowing portfolios (leveraged).
  • If the borrowing rate is above rf, the line kinks and gets flatter to the right of M.
  • Holding the market portfolio = passive management. Deviating from it = active management.

2.2 Systematic vs nonsystematic risk

Systematic (market) risk

Affects the whole market: interest rates, recessions, inflation. Can't be diversified away, so it's priced (you earn a return for bearing it). Measured by β.

Nonsystematic (unique) risk

Specific to one company or industry: a lawsuit, a failed product. Diversifiable, so it's not priced. No reward for holding it.

Total risk decomposition
σi² = βi²σm² + σε²
Total = systematic + nonsystematic.
Splitting a stock's risk
A stock with σ = 40%, market σ = 20%. Change β to see how much of its variance is systematic.
Systematic: β²σm² (priced)Nonsystematic: σε² (not priced)

2.3 Return-generating models and beta

Multifactor model
E(Ri) − Rf = Σ βij · E(Fj)
Fama–French: market, size (SMB), value (HML). Carhart adds momentum.
Market model (single index)
Ri = αi + βiRm + εi
Regression of a stock's returns on the market's.
Beta
βi = Cov(Ri, Rm)σm² = ρi,mσiσm
Market β = 1, risk-free β = 0. Portfolio β = weighted average of betas.
Trap. Beta divides by σm² (variance) in the covariance version, but by σm in the correlation version. Mixing them up is a classic wrong answer.

2.4 CAPM and the security market line

CAPM assumptions

  • Investors are risk-averse, utility-maximizing and rational
  • Markets are frictionless: no taxes or transaction costs
  • Single-period horizon for everyone
  • Homogeneous expectations
  • Assets are infinitely divisible
  • Competitive markets: investors are price takers
SML (CAPM)
E(Ri) = Rf + βi[E(Rm) − Rf]
E(Rm) − Rf is the market risk premium, the slope of the SML. Works for any asset or portfolio, efficient or not.
Security selection with the SML
Enter a stock's beta and your forecast return. Above the SML = undervalued (buy); below = overvalued (sell).

Total risk. Excess return per unit of σ.

Learning module 3

Portfolio management: an overview

The portfolio approach, the investment process, types of investors and the asset management industry.

3.1 The portfolio approach

Judge investments by how they affect the whole portfolio, not one at a time. Diversification lowers risk without necessarily lowering return.

Diversification ratio
Risk of an equal-weighted portfolioRisk of a single security chosen at random
Lower = more diversification benefit.
Limit. Diversification can fail in a crisis: correlations rise toward 1 when markets crash, just when you need diversification most.

3.2 The investment management process

1 · Planning
  • Understand the client's needs
  • Write the IPS
  • Form capital market expectations
  • Set the strategic asset allocation
2 · Execution
  • Asset allocation
  • Security analysis (top-down or bottom-up)
  • Portfolio construction and trading
3 · Feedback
  • Monitoring and rebalancing
  • Performance measurement and reporting

Defined benefit (DB) plan

Employer promises a set benefit. Employer bears the investment risk.

Defined contribution (DC) plan

Employer contributes a set amount; the payout depends on performance. Employee bears the investment risk.

3.4 The asset management industry

Active vs passive

Active tries to beat a benchmark; passive tracks it at low cost. Assets keep shifting toward passive.

Traditional vs alternative

Traditional: stocks, bonds, cash. Alternative: hedge funds, private equity, real estate, commodities, infrastructure. Alternatives charge higher fees.

Buy side vs sell side

Buy side manages money (asset managers). Sell side sells services to them (brokers, banks, research).

Learning module 4

Basics of portfolio planning and construction

The investment policy statement, asset allocation and ESG.

4.1 The investment policy statement (IPS)

A written plan agreed between client and manager. Its main purpose: it guides every investment decision and helps the client stick to the plan. Typical parts: introduction, statement of purpose, duties and responsibilities, procedures, investment objectives, investment constraints, investment guidelines, evaluation and review, and appendices (asset allocation, rebalancing).

Objectives: return and risk

  • Return: absolute (e.g. 7%) or relative to a benchmark. Distinguish required return (what's needed) from desired.
  • Risk: absolute measures (σ, VaR) or relative (tracking error vs a benchmark).
  • Ability to take risk is objective: horizon, wealth, income, needs.
  • Willingness to take risk is subjective: attitudes, questionnaires, past behavior.

Constraints: remember "TTLLU"

  • Time horizon
  • Tax concerns
  • Liquidity needs
  • Legal and regulatory factors
  • Unique circumstances (ESG preferences, restrictions)
Ability vs willingness to take risk
Low willingnessHigh willingness
Low abilityBelow-average riskUse the lower (ability wins)
Educate the client
High abilityUsually the lower
Discuss and educate first		Above-average risk

When ability and willingness conflict, the conservative choice usually wins. Ability can never be overridden by willingness.

4.2 Asset allocation and construction

Strategic asset allocation

Long-term target weights for each asset class. Built from the IPS and capital market expectations. Explains most of the variation in portfolio returns over time.

Tactical asset allocation

Short-term deliberate deviations from the strategic weights to exploit expected opportunities.

Security selection

Deviating from benchmark weights within an asset class.

Rebalancing

Returning to target weights, often when drift goes beyond a set range (corridor).

Risk budgeting

Deciding how much total risk to take and allocating it to where it's expected to be rewarded best.

Core-satellite

A passive or low-cost core plus active satellite positions around it.

Defining asset classes. Assets within a class should be similar (homogeneous); classes should not overlap (mutually exclusive) and should have low correlations with each other so the allocation diversifies.

Learning module 5

The behavioral biases of individuals

Classification questions are near-free points once you know which bucket each bias goes in.

Cognitive errors

Faulty reasoning, statistics or memory. Easier to fix: better information, education and advice can moderate them.

Emotional biases

Come from feelings and impulses. Harder to fix: usually you adapt to them rather than correct them.

5.1 Cognitive: belief perseverance

Trap. Overconfidence has a cognitive side (illusion of knowledge) but the curriculum classifies it as emotional. Illusion of control is cognitive.

5.4 Behavioral finance and market anomalies

  • Momentum: trend-chasing and herding, linked to availability and regret.
  • Bubbles and crashes: overconfidence, confirmation, self-attribution; then panic selling.
  • Value vs growth: the halo effect (good company ⇒ good stock, a form of representativeness) makes growth stocks overpriced. Home bias favors domestic stocks.
Learning module 6

Introduction to risk management

The point is to take the right risks in the right amounts, not to minimize risk.

6.1 The risk management framework

Governance

Top-down oversight by the board: sets risk tolerance and structure.

Identify and measure

Find and quantify the risks being taken.

Infrastructure, policies

Systems, data, people and procedures.

Monitor, mitigate, communicate

Track exposures, adjust them, report them; strategic analysis.

Risk tolerance

Which risks the organization is willing to take, and how much. Set by governance. The starting point.

Risk budgeting

Allocating the total risk allowed across activities or asset classes (e.g. by β, σ or VaR).

Risk exposure

How much an outcome is affected by a risk factor.

6.2 Types of risk

Financial risks

  • Market: prices, rates, FX, commodities
  • Credit: counterparty doesn't pay
  • Liquidity: can't sell without a big price concession

Non-financial risks

  • Settlement, legal, compliance (regulatory, accounting, tax)
  • Model risk, tail risk
  • Operational (people, systems, cyber), solvency
  • Sovereign and political
  • For individuals: mortality, longevity, morbidity (health)
Interactions. Risks are linked: a market drop can trigger margin calls (liquidity), counterparty failures (credit) and solvency problems at once. Questions often ask which risk a scenario also creates.

6.3 Measuring risk

Asset or exposureMeasureSensitivity to
Any portfolioStandard deviationTotal volatility

Value at risk (VaR)
"There's a 5% chance of losing at least $1M in one day."
Minimum loss at a given probability over a given period. Not the maximum loss.
Conditional VaR (expected shortfall)
Average loss when the loss exceeds VaR.
Also: scenario analysis and stress testing for events VaR misses.

6.4 Modifying risk

MethodHowExample
Prevention and avoidanceDon't take the risk at allNot entering a risky market

Choosing. Pick the method with the best cost–benefit. Insurance = transfer; derivatives = shifting.

Practice questions

Work each one before opening the answer.

1An investment has an expected return of 10% and σ of 20%. For an investor with A = 4, the utility is closest to: A. 2% B. 6% C. −70%Show answer
A. U = 0.10 − ½ × 4 × 0.20² = 0.10 − 0.08 = 0.02. Choice C is what you get using percent instead of decimals.
2The utility of a T-bill yielding 3% is: A. higher for more risk-averse investors B. 3% for all investors C. zero for risk-neutral investorsShow answer
B. σ = 0, so the risk penalty is zero for every A and U = 3%.
360% in asset 1 (σ = 20%) and 40% in asset 2 (σ = 30%), ρ = 0.3. Portfolio σ is closest to: A. 21.5% B. 24.0% C. 4.6%Show answer
A. Variance = 0.36(0.04) + 0.16(0.09) + 2(0.6)(0.4)(0.3)(0.2)(0.3) = 0.0144 + 0.0144 + 0.01728 = 0.04608. √0.04608 = 21.47%. B is the weighted average of the σs (ρ = 1). C is the variance, not the σ.
4Average variance of stocks is 0.04 and average covariance is 0.01. For a very large equal-weighted portfolio, σ is closest to: A. 0% B. 10% C. 20%Show answer
B. As N → ∞, variance → average covariance = 0.01, so σ = 10%. Covariance risk can't be diversified away.
5Rf = 2%, E(Ri) = 10%, σi = 20%, A = 4. The optimal weight in the risky portfolio is: A. 25% B. 50% C. 100%Show answer
B. w* = (0.10 − 0.02) / (4 × 0.04) = 0.08 / 0.16 = 0.50.
6Covariance of a stock with the market is 0.0144 and market σ is 15%. The stock's beta is: A. 0.10 B. 0.64 C. 0.96Show answer
B. β = 0.0144 / 0.15² = 0.0144 / 0.0225 = 0.64. C divides by σ instead of σ².
7Rf = 3%, E(Rm) = 9%, β = 1.5. You forecast a 13% return. The stock is: A. overvalued B. fairly valued C. undervaluedShow answer
C. Required = 3% + 1.5 × 6% = 12%. Forecast 13% plots above the SML, so it's undervalued.
8To evaluate one manager who runs a small part of a large, well-diversified pension fund, the most appropriate measure is: A. Sharpe ratio B. M² C. Treynor ratioShow answer
C. In a diversified whole, only systematic risk (β) matters. Sharpe and M² use total risk.
9Which line uses beta as its measure of risk? A. CAL B. CML C. SMLShow answer
C. The CAL and CML use σ (total risk).
10A client invested more just before a strong year. Compared with the time-weighted return, the money-weighted return will be: A. higher B. lower C. the sameShow answer
A. MWR rewards having more money invested in good periods. TWR ignores cash-flow timing.
11An investor refuses to sell a stock until it gets back to what they paid. This is best described as: A. anchoring B. availability C. status quoShow answer
A. Anchoring on the purchase price (a cognitive error). If the question stresses the pain of realizing a loss, the answer would be loss aversion (emotional).
12Which investor typically has the highest liquidity needs? A. Endowment B. Bank C. Defined benefit pension planShow answer
B. Banks must meet deposit withdrawals. Endowments and DB plans have long horizons and low liquidity needs.
13A client has high ability but low willingness to take risk. The adviser should most likely: A. set a high-risk allocation B. discuss and educate, likely adopting the lower risk level C. ignore willingnessShow answer
B. Conflicts are usually resolved toward the more conservative level, after educating the client.
14Buying put options on a stock position is an example of: A. risk transfer B. risk shifting C. risk acceptanceShow answer
B. Derivatives shift the distribution of outcomes. Insurance would be risk transfer.
