Exam game plan

What candidates who passed wish they'd known going in.

What actually gets tested

  • Fixed income is one of the most calculation-heavy topics: bond prices, yields, forward rates, duration and convexity estimates.
  • The concept questions are about direction: what happens to price, duration or spread when yields, options or credit quality change. If you can draw the price–yield curve, you can answer most of them.
  • Securitization and MBS questions are mostly vocabulary: tranches, credit enhancement, contraction vs extension risk.
  • Three choices per question and no penalty for guessing, so never leave one blank.

Highest-yield items, ranked

  1. Bond pricing and the price–yield relationship (premium, par, discount)
  2. Duration: Macaulay, modified, effective, money duration, PVBP
  3. Price change estimate with duration and convexity
  4. Spot, par and forward rates
  5. Callable, putable and convertible bonds; effect on duration and convexity
  6. Yield spreads: G-spread, I-spread, Z-spread, OAS
  7. Credit risk: expected loss, seniority, ratings, spread changes
  8. Securitization: tranching and credit enhancement
  9. MBS prepayment: contraction vs extension risk, CPR and PSA, CMOs
  10. Full vs flat price, accrued interest, money market yields
Top traps. Using semiannual durations without annualizing (divide duration by the periodicity, convexity by its square). Using Macaulay duration in a price-change formula (use modified or effective). Forgetting the convexity term is added for an option-free bond. Mixing up full and flat price. Confusing discount-basis and add-on money market yields. Calling falling rates "extension risk" for an MBS (it's contraction risk).
BA II Plus. Bond price: 2nd CLR TVM, then N, I/Y, PMT, FV and CPT PV. Semiannual bond: double N, halve I/Y and PMT. Yield: enter price as a negative PV, then CPT I/Y (double it for a semiannual bond's annual yield). Pricing with spot rates: discount each cash flow separately; TVM only works with one rate.
Part 1 · Learning modules 1–2

Bond features, cash flows and types

What a bond is made of, how it pays, and the options built into it.

1.1 Basic features

Issuer

Corporates, governments (sovereign and non-sovereign), quasi-government agencies, supranationals.

Maturity (tenor)

When principal is repaid. Money market: 1 year or less. Capital market: over 1 year. Perpetual bonds never mature.

Par value

Principal repaid at maturity (face value). Prices are quoted per 100 of par.

Coupon rate and frequency

Annual interest as a % of par, paid annually, semiannually, quarterly or monthly. Zero-coupon bonds pay none and sell at a discount.

Currency

Dual-currency bonds pay coupons in one currency and principal in another. Currency option bonds let the investor choose.

Yield

The return the market requires. It sets the price, not the coupon.

1.2 Indenture, covenants and seniority

Indenture (trust deed)

The legal contract: issuer's obligations, investors' rights, source of repayment, collateral, credit enhancements and covenants. A trustee monitors it on the bondholders' behalf.

Covenants

Affirmative = what the issuer must do (pay interest, maintain insurance, comply with laws). Negative = what it must not do (take on more debt, pledge assets to others (negative pledge), sell key assets, pay large dividends).

Secured bonds are backed by collateral (e.g. mortgage bonds, equipment trust certificates); unsecured bonds (debentures) rely on the issuer's general credit. Secured ranks ahead in a default and has higher expected recovery.

1.3 Legal, regulatory and tax

TypeWhat it is
Domestic bondIssued by a local issuer, in the local currency, in the local market
Foreign bondIssued by a foreign issuer in another country's market and currency (Yankee in the US, Samurai in Japan, Panda in China, Bulldog in the UK)
EurobondIssued outside the jurisdiction of its currency (e.g. a USD bond sold in London); lighter regulation; historically bearer form
Global bondIssued at the same time in the Eurobond market and at least one domestic market
  • Bearer vs registered: bearer bonds are owned by whoever holds them; registered bonds record the owner.
  • Tax: coupons are usually taxed as income; capital gains are taxed on sale. Original issue discount (OID) bonds may be taxed on the annual accretion. Some government bonds (e.g. US municipals) are tax-exempt.

1.4 Principal repayment structures

Bullet

Coupons only, all principal at maturity. Most common.

Fully amortizing

Each payment includes interest and principal; balance hits zero at maturity (like a mortgage).

Partially amortizing

Some principal paid along the way, with a balloon payment at maturity.

Sinking fund

Issuer retires part of the issue each year. Lowers credit risk, but investors face reinvestment risk and may be repaid below market price.

1.5 Coupon structures

StructureHow it works
Floating-rate note (FRN)Coupon = market reference rate (e.g. SOFR) + quoted margin, reset periodically. A cap limits the coupon (benefits the issuer); a floor sets a minimum (benefits the investor). Inverse floaters move opposite to the reference rate.
Step-up couponCoupon rises on set dates; often paired with a call so the issuer is pushed to refinance

1.6 Embedded options (contingency provisions)

Callable bond

Issuer can repay early, usually when rates fall. Investors demand a higher yield. Terms: call price and schedule, call protection (lockout) period, make-whole call. American (any time), European (one date), Bermudan (set dates).

Putable bond

Investor can sell back to the issuer at the put price, usually when rates rise. Investors accept a lower yield.

Convertible bond

Investor can convert into shares. Lower yield than a straight bond. Contingent convertibles (CoCos) convert automatically if a trigger is hit, such as a bank's capital ratio falling.

Who owns the option? Call = issuer, so it lowers the bond's value to investors. Put and conversion = investor, so they raise it. Bond value: callable = straight − call; putable = straight + put.
Part 2 · Learning modules 3–5

Issuance, trading and markets

How bonds are sold and traded, and how corporations and governments borrow.

2.1 Primary and secondary markets

Primary market (new issues)

  • Underwritten offering: the bank buys the whole issue and resells it, taking the risk.
  • Best-efforts offering: the bank sells what it can, no guarantee.
  • Auction: common for government bonds.
  • Shelf registration: one registration, sold in pieces over time.
  • Private placement: sold directly to a few investors; not registered, less liquid, custom covenants.

Secondary market (trading)

  • Mostly over-the-counter through dealers, not exchanges.
  • Liquidity is measured by the bid–offer spread: narrower = more liquid.
  • Government bonds are the most liquid; small corporate issues the least.
  • Settlement is usually T+1 or T+2.

2.2 Corporate issuers

Short-term funding

  • Bank lines of credit: uncommitted (bank can refuse), committed (bank must lend, fee charged), revolving credit (multi-year, most reliable).
  • Secured loans and factoring: borrowing against or selling receivables.
  • Commercial paper: unsecured short-term notes sold at a discount, by strong issuers. Rollover risk: can't refinance at maturity, so it's backed by bank backup lines.

Long-term funding

  • Investment-grade issuers: longer maturities, fewer covenants, often unsecured.
  • High-yield issuers: more covenants, often secured, shorter maturities, often callable.
  • Medium-term notes: issued continuously in a range of maturities.
  • Spreading out maturities (laddering) reduces refinancing risk.

2.3 Repurchase agreements (repos)

A repo is a short-term secured loan: one party sells a bond and agrees to buy it back later at a higher price. The difference is the interest. The other side is a reverse repo.

2.4 Government issuers

Sovereign bonds

Bills (≤ 1 year, sold at a discount), notes and bonds. On-the-run = the most recent issue: most liquid, used as the benchmark, slightly lower yield than off-the-run.

Auctions

Single-price: all winning bidders pay the same price (at the highest accepted yield). Multiple-price: each winner pays its own bid. Non-competitive bids accept whatever yield is set.

Local vs foreign currency

A government can tax or print its own currency, so local-currency debt is usually rated higher than its foreign-currency debt.

Non-sovereign

States, provinces and cities. General obligation (GO) bonds are backed by taxing power; revenue bonds by a project's income, so they're riskier.

Agency / quasi-government

Issued by government-sponsored entities; often an implied (not explicit) government guarantee.

Supranational

Multilateral bodies such as the World Bank and regional development banks; usually very highly rated.

Bond prices and yields

Pricing, the price–yield relationship, accrued interest, yield measures, spreads, floaters and money market yields.

3.1 Pricing a bond

Price = present value of all cash flows at the market discount rate r
PV = PMT(1 + r)1 + PMT(1 + r)2 + … + PMT + FV(1 + r)N
With spot rates, discount each cash flow at its own rate: PMT/(1+z1) + PMT/(1+z2)² + …
Premium coupon rate > market yield → price > 100
Par coupon rate = market yield → price = 100
Discount coupon rate < market yield → price < 100
Four price–yield rules. (1) Price and yield move in opposite directions. (2) For the same yield change, price rises more than it falls (convexity). (3) Lower-coupon bonds are more sensitive to yield changes. (4) Longer-maturity bonds are usually more sensitive.

3.2 Full price, flat price and accrued interest

Full (dirty) price
PVFull = PVFlat + AI
What the buyer actually pays.
Accrued interest

Full price between coupons

  • Flat (clean) price is what's quoted. It avoids the jump in price on each coupon date.
  • Day counts: government bonds usually use actual/actual; corporate bonds usually use 30/360.
  • Matrix pricing: to price a bond that rarely trades, use yields of comparable bonds (similar maturity, coupon and credit), interpolating if needed.

3.3 Yield measures

Yield to maturity
The single r that makes PV = price
An IRR. Assumes held to maturity, all payments made, coupons reinvested at the YTM.
Periodicity conversion
(1 + APRmm)m = (1 + APRnn)n
e.g. 6% semiannual = 1.03² − 1 = 6.09% annual.
Current yield
Annual couponFlat price
Ignores reinvestment and gains or losses.
  • Street convention: assumes payments arrive on scheduled dates. True yield: uses actual dates (weekends, holidays).
  • Simple yield: (coupon + straight-line amortized gain or loss) / flat price.
  • Yield to call: uses the call date and call price. Yield to worst: the lowest of YTM and all yields to call.
  • Annual-pay vs semiannual: to compare, convert to the same periodicity first.

3.4 Yield spreads

SpreadMeasured overNotes
Benchmark / G-spreadGovernment bond yield of the same maturity (interpolated if needed)Simplest; uses one point on the curve
I-spreadSwap rate of the same maturityCommon for corporate and bank bonds

A spread compensates for credit risk, liquidity risk and tax differences relative to the benchmark.

3.5 Floating-rate notes

FRN coupon
Coupon = MRR + Quoted margin
MRR = market reference rate (e.g. SOFR). The quoted margin is fixed at issue.
The discount margin (required margin) is what the market demands now.
QM > DM premium · QM = DM par on reset dates · QM < DM discount.
Because the coupon resets, an FRN's price stays close to par and its duration is short (about the time to the next reset).

3.6 Money market yields

Discount rate basis (e.g. T-bills)
PV = FV × (1 − DaysYear × DR)
Add-on rate basis (e.g. CDs, repos)
PV = FV1 + (Days/Year) × AOR
Converting to add-on
AOR = YearDays × FV − PVPV
Bond equivalent yield = add-on rate on a 365-day year.
Trap. A discount rate understates the true return: it divides the interest by FV, not by the amount you invest, and often uses a 360-day year. Always convert to an add-on (365-day) yield before comparing.
Part 4 · Learning module 9

The term structure: spot, par and forward curves

Three ways to describe the same yield curve, and how to move between them.

Spot rate (z)

The yield on a zero-coupon bond for one maturity. Use it to discount a single cash flow at that date.

Par rate

The coupon rate that makes a bond price exactly 100 when discounted at the spot rates.

Forward rate

A rate agreed today for borrowing in the future. "1y1y" = a 1-year rate starting 1 year from now; "2y3y" = a 3-year rate starting in 2 years.

Spot and forward rates
(1 + zB)B = (1 + zA)A × (1 + IFRA,B−A)B−A
Example: z1 = 2%, z2 = 3% → 1y1y = 1.03²/1.02 − 1 = 4.01%.
Par rate from spot rates
p = 1 − dNd1 + d2 + … + dN
dt = 1/(1 + zt)t, the discount factor. Same example: 2-year par rate = 2.985%.
Spot, par and forward curves
Change the level and slope of the spot curve. Each forward point is the 1-year rate starting one year before that maturity.
Spot curvePar curve1-year forward rates
2-year spot
1y1y forward
2-year par
Ordering rule. Upward-sloping curve: forward > spot > par. Downward-sloping: forward < spot < par. Flat: all three are equal. The par curve sits closest to "average", the forward curve moves the most.
  • Why forwards sit above spots on an upward curve: the 2-year spot is roughly an average of the 1-year spot and the 1y1y forward, so the forward must be higher to pull the average up.
  • Breakeven idea: investing for 2 years at z2 earns the same as 1 year at z1 then rolling at the 1y1y forward.
  • Pricing: a bond priced with spot rates and the same bond priced with its YTM give the same price; the YTM is a kind of weighted average of the spot rates.
Part 5 · Learning modules 10–13

Interest rate risk: duration and convexity

The most tested area of fixed income. Learn what each duration measures and when to use it.

5.1 Sources of return and the investment horizon

Three sources of return

  1. Coupons and principal received
  2. Interest on reinvested coupons
  3. Gain or loss if sold before maturity (vs carrying value)

Two opposing risks

Market price risk: rates up → price down. Matters for short horizons.
Reinvestment risk: rates down → coupons reinvested at less. Matters for long horizons.

Duration gap
Macaulay duration − Investment horizon
Positive gap: price risk dominates (hurt by rising rates). Negative gap: reinvestment risk dominates (hurt by falling rates).

Duration is higher when…

  • Maturity is longer (usually)
  • Coupon is lower (more value arrives at the end)
  • Yield is lower (distant cash flows are discounted less)
  • There's no embedded option (calls and puts shorten effective duration)

Special cases

  • Zero-coupon bond: MacDur = maturity
  • Perpetuity: MacDur = (1 + r)/r
  • Floating-rate note: about the time to the next reset
  • Between coupon dates: duration falls as time passes, then jumps up after each coupon

5.3 Convexity and the full price-change estimate

Approximate convexity
PV− + PV+ − 2 × PV0(ΔYield)² × PV0
Effective convexity: same with ΔCurve.
% price change
%ΔPVFull ≈ (−AnnModDur × ΔYield) + ½ × AnnConvexity × (ΔYield)²
Use decimals: 50 bp = 0.005. The convexity term is always positive for an option-free bond, so it adds to gains and reduces losses.
  • Higher convexity is better for the investor: the price rises more when yields fall and drops less when they rise.
  • Convexity is higher with longer maturity, lower coupon, lower yield and more spread-out cash flows.
  • Annualizing: AnnModDur = periodic ModDur / periods per year; AnnConvexity = periodic convexity / (periods per year)².

5.6 Portfolios, yield volatility and credit spreads

  • Portfolio duration ≈ market-value-weighted average of the bonds' durations. It assumes a parallel shift in the yield curve.
  • Yield volatility matters too: a bond's price risk depends on duration and how much its yield moves. Short-term yields are usually more volatile than long-term yields.
  • Credit spread changes use the same formula, with ΔSpread in place of ΔYield: %ΔPV ≈ −ModDur × ΔSpread + ½ × Convexity × (ΔSpread)².

Default risk vs spread risk

Default risk: POD and loss severity. Spread risk: the bond's price falls because its spread widens, from credit migration (a downgrade) or market liquidity risk (wider bid–offer spreads).

The Cs of credit analysis

Capacity to pay (cash flow), capital (cushion of equity and assets), collateral (assets pledged), covenants (terms protecting lenders), plus character (management's track record and integrity).

6.4 What drives credit spreads

  • Credit cycle and economy: spreads widen in downturns and narrow in expansions.
  • Market conditions: falling stock markets and higher volatility widen spreads.
  • Dealer capital and liquidity: less market-making capacity means wider spreads.
  • Supply and demand: heavy new issuance can widen spreads.
  • High-yield spreads move much more than investment-grade spreads.

Part 7 · Learning modules 17–19

Securitization, ABS and MBS

Pooling loans into bonds, splitting the risk into tranches, and prepayment risk.

7.1 How securitization works

1 · Borrowers

Take out mortgages, auto loans, credit card balances.

2 · Originator (seller)

Makes the loans, then sells the pool.

3 · SPE (issuer)

A bankruptcy-remote entity that buys the pool and issues the securities.

4 · Investors

Buy tranches and receive the pool's payments.

The servicer collects payments and handles delinquencies; the trustee protects investors' interests. Because the SPE is separate, investors are protected if the originator goes bankrupt.

Benefits

  • Lower funding costs for originators
  • Frees up bank capital to make more loans
  • Investors get access to loan pools with the risk level they choose
  • More liquid than individual loans

Covered bonds vs ABS

  • Covered bond assets stay on the bank's balance sheet in a cover pool
  • Dual recourse: investors can claim on the bank and the pool
  • Dynamic pool: bad assets must be replaced
  • So covered bonds usually have lower credit risk and yields

7.2 Tranching and credit enhancement

Credit tranching (subordination): losses hit the lowest tranche first and work up, the "waterfall". Time tranching: splits prepayment risk by paying principal to tranches in sequence.

Internal credit enhancement

  • Subordination (the tranche structure)
  • Overcollateralization: pool worth more than the bonds issued

  • Reserve accounts: cash set aside for losses

  • Third-party guarantees

7.3 Asset-backed securities

Auto loan ABS

Amortizing: principal paid down monthly, plus prepayments when cars are sold or refinanced.

Credit card ABS

Non-amortizing. During the lockout (revolving) period, principal is reinvested in new receivables; investors get interest only. Then principal is paid out. Early amortization triggers start repayment early if the pool performs badly.

Collateralized debt obligations

Backed by a pool of debt run by a collateral manager: CLOs (leveraged loans, most common), CBOs (bonds). The equity tranche gets what's left after the debt tranches are paid.

7.4 Residential mortgages and MBS

Mortgage features

  • Rate: fixed, adjustable (ARM), hybrid
  • Amortization: full, partial (balloon), interest-only
  • Prepayment penalties (some markets)
  • Recourse (lender can claim the borrower's other assets) vs non-recourse (only the property)
  • Loan-to-value (LTV): lower LTV = lower default risk

Agency vs non-agency RMBS

  • Agency: guaranteed by a government agency or GSE (e.g. Ginnie Mae, Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac); loans must meet standards (conforming)
  • Non-agency: no guarantee, so they need credit enhancement and carry credit risk

Contraction risk

Rates fall → borrowers refinance → prepayments speed up → the MBS is repaid sooner, and the money must be reinvested at lower rates. Price gains are limited (negative convexity, like a callable bond).

Extension risk

Rates rise → prepayments slow → the MBS lasts longer just when its price is falling and you'd rather have cash to reinvest at higher rates.

Sequential-payTranche A gets all principal first, then B, then C. Short tranches have less extension risk; long tranches have less contraction risk.
PAC tranche (planned amortization class)Follows a set principal schedule as long as prepayments stay within a range (the PAC collar). Most predictable cash flows.
Support (companion) trancheAbsorbs prepayment surprises so the PAC can stay on schedule. Most prepayment risk, so the highest yield.
Floater / inverse floaterA fixed-rate tranche split into one that rises with rates and one that falls.
Trap. A CMO doesn't remove prepayment risk; it only moves it between tranches. The PAC is protected because the support tranche takes the hit.

7.6 Commercial MBS

Debt service coverage ratio
DSCR = Net operating incomeDebt service
Above 1 = property income covers the payments. Higher is safer.
Loan-to-value
LTV = Loan amountProperty value
Lower is safer.

Key differences from RMBS

Loans are usually non-recourse, so credit analysis focuses on the property's income. Strong call protection limits prepayment risk. Balloon risk: the borrower can't refinance the final balloon, which extends the loan (a form of extension risk).

Call protection. At the loan level: prepayment lockout, defeasance, prepayment penalty points, yield maintenance charges. At the structure level: sequential credit tranches.

Practice questions

Work each one before opening the answer.

1A 5-year bond pays a 6% annual coupon. If the market discount rate is 5%, its price per 100 par is closest to: A. 95.90 B. 100.00 C. 104.33Show answer
C. N = 5, I/Y = 5, PMT = 6, FV = 100 → PV = 104.33. Coupon above yield means a premium. A is the price at a 7% yield.
2A bond's coupon rate is below its market discount rate. The bond trades at: A. a premium B. par C. a discountShow answer
C. Investors need extra return beyond the coupon, which comes from buying below par.
3A 90-day T-bill is quoted at a 4% discount rate on a 360-day year. Its price per 100 is: A. 96.00 B. 99.00 C. 99.01Show answer
B. 100 × (1 − 90/360 × 0.04) = 100 × 0.99 = 99.00. C uses the add-on formula (100/1.01). A forgets the 90/360.
4The 1-year spot rate is 2% and the 2-year spot rate is 3%. The 1y1y forward rate is closest to: A. 2.50% B. 3.00% C. 4.01%Show answer
C. 1.03² / 1.02 − 1 = 1.0609/1.02 − 1 = 4.01%.
5The spot curve slopes upward. For the same maturity, which rate is lowest? A. Par rate B. Spot rate C. Forward rateShow answer
A. Upward-sloping: forward > spot > par.
6A 3-year bond pays a 5% annual coupon and yields 5%. Its Macaulay duration is closest to: A. 2.72 B. 2.86 C. 3.00Show answer
B. PVs: 4.762, 4.535, 90.703 (sum 100). Weighted: (1 × 4.762 + 2 × 4.535 + 3 × 90.703)/100 = 2.86. A is the modified duration (2.86/1.05). C would be a zero-coupon bond.
7Annual modified duration is 7 and annual convexity is 60. If the yield rises 100 bp, the price change is closest to: A. −7.30% B. −7.00% C. −6.70%Show answer
C. −7 × 0.01 + ½ × 60 × 0.01² = −0.07 + 0.003 = −6.70%. A subtracts the convexity term instead of adding it.
8A bond is priced at 100. If yields fall 25 bp its price is 102.50; if they rise 25 bp it's 97.60. Approximate modified duration is closest to: A. 4.9 B. 9.8 C. 19.6Show answer
B. (102.50 − 97.60) / (2 × 0.0025 × 100) = 4.90 / 0.5 = 9.8. C forgets the 2 in the denominator.
9All yield 5%. Which has the highest duration? A. 10-year, 3% coupon B. 10-year, 6% coupon C. 5-year, 3% couponShow answer
A. Longer maturity and lower coupon both raise duration.
10An investor's horizon is 4 years and the bond's Macaulay duration is 6. The investor is most exposed to: A. rising rates B. falling rates C. neitherShow answer
A. Duration gap = +2, so price risk dominates: they must sell before the bond's duration, and rising rates lower the sale price.
11The most appropriate duration measure for a callable bond is: A. Macaulay B. modified C. effectiveShow answer
C. Its cash flows change with rates, so you need a curve-based measure.
12Compared with its Z-spread, a callable bond's OAS is: A. higher B. lower C. the sameShow answer
B. OAS = Z-spread − the call's value (in spread terms). Part of the callable bond's extra spread is just pay for the call, not credit risk.
13When yields fall well below a callable bond's coupon rate, the bond most likely shows: A. positive convexity B. negative convexity C. zero durationShow answer
B. The price is capped near the call price, so it rises less than it would fall.
14Probability of default is 2% and recovery rate is 40%. Expected loss as a % of exposure is: A. 0.8% B. 1.2% C. 2.0%Show answer
B. 2% × (1 − 0.40) = 1.2%. A uses the recovery rate instead of the loss.
15Which ranks highest in priority of claims? A. Senior unsecured B. Second lien C. Senior subordinatedShow answer
B. Secured debt (first, then second lien) ranks ahead of all unsecured debt.
16A bond's modified duration is 6. If its credit spread widens 50 bp, ignoring convexity, its price changes by about: A. −0.3% B. −3.0% C. −30%Show answer
B. −6 × 0.005 = −3.0%.
17A 100M pool has a 10M subordinated tranche, 15M mezzanine and 75M senior. Pool losses are 12M. The mezzanine tranche loses: A. 0 B. 2M C. 12MShow answer
B. The subordinated tranche absorbs the first 10M; the next 2M hits mezzanine. Senior loses nothing.
18Which is an external credit enhancement? A. Overcollateralization B. Excess spread C. Letter of creditShow answer
C. A and B are internal (built into the deal's structure).
19Mortgage rates fall sharply. An MBS investor faces: A. extension risk B. contraction risk C. no prepayment riskShow answer
B. Borrowers refinance, so principal comes back early and is reinvested at lower rates.
20A mortgage pool's SMM is 0.5%. Its CPR is closest to: A. 0.50% B. 5.84% C. 6.00%Show answer
B. 1 − (1 − 0.005)12 = 1 − 0.9416 = 5.84%. C multiplies by 12, which overstates it because of compounding.
21In a CMO, which tranche absorbs most of the prepayment risk? A. PAC tranche B. Support tranche C. Senior sequential trancheShow answer
B. The support (companion) tranche takes prepayment surprises so the PAC stays on schedule.
22Compared with an ABS, a covered bond typically has: A. dual recourse B. assets removed from the bank's balance sheet C. a static poolShow answer
A. Investors have a claim on the issuing bank and on the cover pool, which stays on the balance sheet and is dynamic.
23A $1,000 par convertible has a conversion ratio of 25 and the stock trades at $36. The conversion value is: A. $900 B. $1,000 C. $1,440Show answer
A. 25 × $36 = $900. The conversion price is $1,000 / 25 = $40.
24An FRN has a quoted margin of 50 bp and the market's discount margin is 70 bp. The FRN trades at: A. a premium B. par C. a discountShow answer
C. It pays less over the reference rate than the market requires.
25Which feature benefits the issuer? A. Put provision B. Call provision C. Coupon floor on an FRNShow answer
B. The issuer can refinance when rates fall. Puts and floors benefit investors.
26Which most likely leads to a higher repo haircut? A. Shorter repo term B. Higher-quality collateral C. Lower credit quality of the borrowerShow answer
C. A riskier borrower means the lender wants more protection. Longer terms and lower-quality collateral also raise haircuts.
