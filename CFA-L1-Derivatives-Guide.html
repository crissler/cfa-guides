Part 1 · Learning modules 1–2

Derivative instruments and markets

A derivative's value comes from an underlying: equities, fixed income and interest rates, currencies, commodities, credit, or others (weather, crypto, inflation). Derivatives are either forward commitments (both sides are obligated) or contingent claims (one side has a right, not an obligation).

Over-the-counter (OTC)

Private, customized contracts between two parties (usually with a dealer). Flexible but less transparent, less liquid, and exposed to counterparty credit risk. Many standard OTC trades must now clear through a central counterparty (CCP).

Exchange-traded

Standardized terms (size, expiry, settlement), traded on an exchange and cleared through a clearinghouse that guarantees performance. Margin and daily settlement limit credit risk. More transparent and liquid.
InstrumentKey features
ForwardOTC agreement to buy/sell at a set price on a future date. No cash at inception (value zero). Settled at maturity by delivery or cash. Counterparty risk.
FuturesExchange-traded, standardized forward. Daily settlement (mark-to-market) through margin accounts: initial margin, maintenance margin, and variation margin that restores the account to the initial level. Most are closed out before expiry.
SwapExchange of a series of cash flows, e.g. fixed vs floating interest on a notional amount. Equivalent to a series of forwards. Usually OTC.
OptionBuyer pays a premium for the right to buy (call) or sell (put) at the exercise (strike) price. European: exercise only at expiry; American: any time up to expiry.
Credit derivativeE.g. a credit default swap (CDS): the protection buyer pays periodic premiums; the protection seller pays if a credit event (e.g. default) occurs on the reference entity.

Part 2 · Learning module 3

Benefits, risks, and issuer and investor uses

Benefits

  • Risk allocation and transfer: move risk to those willing to bear it
  • Information discovery: futures and options prices reveal expectations and volatility
  • Operational advantages: low transaction costs, easy shorting, high liquidity, leverage (small cash outlay)
  • Market efficiency: mispricing is cheaper to exploit

Risks

  • High implicit leverage: large exposure for little cash
  • Lack of transparency and complexity
  • Basis risk: the hedge and the exposure don't move exactly together
  • Liquidity risk: margin calls need cash
  • Counterparty credit risk (OTC)
  • Destabilization and systemic risk from excessive speculation
Hedge accounting (issuers)What's hedgedExample
Cash flow hedgeVariability of future cash flowsSwap floating-rate debt into fixed (receive floating, pay fixed)
Fair value hedgeChanges in the value of a fixed asset or liabilitySwap fixed-rate debt into floating; hedge commodity inventory
Net investment hedgeCurrency risk on a foreign subsidiaryFX forward or cross-currency swap

Hedge accounting lets the gains and losses on the derivative and the hedged item be recognized together, reducing earnings volatility. Investors use derivatives to gain or modify exposure (e.g. change portfolio duration or beta), replicate a cash position cheaply, hedge, or speculate.

Part 3 · Learning modules 4–6

Arbitrage, cost of carry, and pricing forwards and futures

No-arbitrage pricing

Arbitrage = a riskless profit with no net investment. Two portfolios with identical future cash flows must have the same price today (replication). Derivatives are priced so that no arbitrage exists, discounting at the risk-free rate (risk neutrality), regardless of investors' risk preferences.

Price vs value

A forward's price F0(T) is set at inception so its value is zero. As the underlying moves, the value becomes positive for one side and negative for the other. At expiry, value = ST − F0(T) for the long.
Forward price (no income or costs)
F0(T) = S0(1 + r)T
With income (I) and costs (C)
F0(T) = [S0 − PV(I) + PV(C)](1 + r)T
Continuous: F0(T) = S0e(r + c − i)T. Income (dividends, coupons, convenience yield) lowers the forward price; storage and insurance costs raise it.
Value of a long forward at time t
Vt(T) = St − F0(T)(1 + r)−(T − t)
Adjust St for the PV of remaining income or costs if there are any.

3.1 Interest rate forwards (FRAs)

Implied forward rate
(1 + zB)B = (1 + zA)A(1 + IFRA,B−A)B−A
The no-arbitrage rate for borrowing from A to B, implied by today's spot curve.
A forward rate agreement locks in an interest rate for a future period on a notional amount. The long (fixed-rate payer) gains if the market reference rate ends up above the FRA rate. Settlement is the rate difference × notional × period, discounted back to the start of the period.

3.2 Futures vs forwards

Futures are marked to market daily, so gains and losses are realized each day. If futures prices are positively correlated with interest rates, longs reinvest gains at higher rates and finance losses at lower rates, so futures prices exceed forward prices. Negative correlation reverses this. With constant (or uncorrelated) rates, they're equal. After each daily settlement, a futures contract's value resets to zero.

Part 4 · Learning module 7

Pricing and valuing swaps

A swap is a series of forwards

A fixed-for-floating interest rate swap is like a portfolio of FRAs, each locking in the same fixed rate. The fixed (swap) rate is set so the swap's value is zero at inception.
Swap fixed rate (a par rate)
sN = 1 − dNd1 + d2 + … + dN
dt = 1/(1 + zt)t. Same as the par rate on a bond.
Swap rate from the spot curve
A 4-year annual-pay swap. The swap rate is an average of the spot rates, weighted toward earlier years.

Value after inception: if market swap rates rise above the contract's fixed rate, the fixed payer gains: value to the fixed payer ≈ (new swap rate − old swap rate) × notional × sum of remaining discount factors.

Pricing options: bounds, parity and the binomial model

Intrinsic and time value

Option value = intrinsic value (exercise value if positive) + time value. Time value falls to zero at expiry. A European call's lower bound is above its intrinsic value because the strike is paid later.

Synthetic positions

Rearrange parity: synthetic call = S + p − PV(X); synthetic put = c + PV(X) − S; synthetic stock = c − p + PV(X); synthetic bond = S + p − c. If one side is mispriced, buy the cheap side and sell the expensive side.

Firm value as options

Shareholders hold a call on the firm's assets with strike = face value of debt. Debtholders hold a risk-free bond minus a put on the firm's assets. That's why more risk helps equity and hurts debt.
