Exam game plan

What candidates who passed wish they'd known going in.

What actually gets tested

  • FSA is big and detailed. Most questions ask about the effect of an accounting choice: "Compared with FIFO, LIFO gives higher or lower…?" Learn the direction of each effect on net income, assets, cash flow and ratios.
  • IFRS vs US GAAP differences come up again and again. The comparison table near the end is worth memorizing.
  • Calculations are usually short: CFO by the indirect method, EPS, LIFO-to-FIFO, depreciation, deferred taxes, bond interest expense, ratios.
  • FSA is the foundation for corporate issuers, equity valuation and credit analysis, so time spent here pays off elsewhere.

Highest-yield items, ranked

  1. Cash flow statement: classification, indirect method, IFRS vs GAAP choices, FCFF and FCFE
  2. Inventory: FIFO vs LIFO vs average, LIFO reserve, write-downs
  3. Long-lived assets: capitalize vs expense, depreciation, impairment, revaluation
  4. Income taxes: deferred tax liabilities and assets, tax expense
  5. Ratios and DuPont analysis
  6. Revenue recognition, non-recurring items, accounting changes
  7. Basic and diluted EPS
  8. Bonds (effective interest), leases, pensions
  9. Financial reporting quality and warning signs
  10. Conceptual framework, audit opinions, OCI
Top traps. Mixing up the sign of working-capital changes in the indirect method (an increase in an asset reduces CFO). Forgetting to subtract gains on asset sales from CFO. Treating LIFO as allowed under IFRS (it isn't). Adding the full LIFO reserve change to net income (adjust it after tax). Using book value instead of fair value to compute goodwill. Calling a change in useful life "retrospective" (estimates change prospectively).

Part 1 · Learning module 1

Framework, statements and reporting standards

What the statements are, where else to look, and the rules behind them.

1.1 How the statements connect

The statements are linked
Net income flows into equity and is the starting point for cash from operations; the cash flow statement explains the change in the balance sheet's cash.
Income statement

Revenue − expenses = net income for the period.

Statement of changes in equity

Opening retained earnings + net income − dividends = closing retained earnings. Also shows OCI and share issues.

Balance sheet

Assets = liabilities + equity at a point in time. Equity includes the closing retained earnings.

Cash flow statement

Starts from net income (indirect method); ends with the change in cash that ties to the balance sheet.

Beyond the statements

  • Notes: accounting policies, estimates, segment data, commitments
  • MD&A (management commentary): results, trends, risks, liquidity
  • Auditor's report
  • Proxy statements, earnings calls, industry and economic data

The analysis process

  1. Define the purpose and context
  2. Collect data
  3. Process data (adjust, compute ratios)
  4. Analyze and interpret
  5. Communicate conclusions
  6. Follow up and update

Audit opinions

  • Unqualified (clean) statements are fairly presented
  • Qualified fair except for a specific issue
  • Adverse materially misstated, not fairly presented
  • Disclaimer auditor can't express an opinion

Reports also list key audit matters (critical audit matters in the US).

1.2 Standards and the conceptual framework

Who sets the rules

Standard setters write the standards: the IASB (IFRS) and the FASB (US GAAP). Regulators enforce them and have legal authority: e.g. the SEC in the US; IOSCO coordinates securities regulators globally.

Qualitative characteristics

Fundamental: relevance (including materiality) and faithful representation (complete, neutral, free from error).
Enhancing: comparability, verifiability, timeliness, understandability.
Constraint: benefits should exceed the cost of reporting.

Elements: assets, liabilities, equity, income, expenses. Key assumptions: accrual basis (record when earned or incurred, not when cash moves) and going concern.

Analyzing income statements

Revenue and expense recognition, non-recurring items, EPS and comprehensive income.

Recognize revenue as each obligation is satisfied (at a point in time or over time)

  • Over time (e.g. long-term construction): recognize in proportion to progress, often measured by costs incurred.
  • Principal vs agent: a principal controls the good or service and reports gross revenue; an agent arranges it for someone else and reports only its net commission.
  • Variable consideration (discounts, bonuses) is included only if a significant reversal is unlikely. Cash received before delivery is a liability (unearned revenue).

2.2 Expenses and non-recurring items

Expense recognition

Match expenses to the revenue they help generate (matching), or recognize them in the period incurred. Choices such as depreciation method, inventory method and bad-debt estimates affect reported profit.

Discontinued operations

A component the company has disposed of or plans to sell. Reported separately, after income from continuing operations, net of tax. Analysts usually exclude it when forecasting.

Unusual or infrequent items

Shown within continuing operations, before tax (e.g. restructuring charges, gains on asset sales). Extraordinary items are no longer allowed under either IFRS or US GAAP.

2.4 Comprehensive income

Comprehensive income
Net income + Other comprehensive income (OCI)
OCI collects gains and losses that bypass the income statement.

Items in OCI

  • Foreign currency translation adjustments
  • Unrealized gains/losses on debt securities at fair value through OCI
  • Pension remeasurements (actuarial gains and losses)
  • Effective portion of cash flow hedges
  • Revaluation surplus on PP&E (IFRS)

Not in OCI: unrealized gains on securities at fair value through profit or loss (they go to net income).

Common-size income statement: every line divided by revenue, giving the margins directly (gross, operating, pretax, net).

Part 3 · Learning module 3

Analyzing balance sheets

Classification, measurement, intangibles and goodwill, financial instruments and equity.

Current vs non-current

Current assets are expected to be used up or turned into cash within one year or the operating cycle (whichever is longer); current liabilities are due within that time. A liquidity-based presentation (no current/non-current split) is allowed under IFRS when more relevant, e.g. for banks.

Measurement bases

Historical cost, amortized cost, fair value, and lower of cost and net realizable value (inventory). The balance sheet mixes them, so book equity rarely equals market value.

Common-size balance sheet

Every item divided by total assets; useful for comparing firms of different sizes.

3.1 Intangibles and goodwill

Goodwill
Purchase price − Fair value of identifiable net assets acquired
Only arises in an acquisition. Internally generated goodwill is never recorded.
Goodwill and indefinite-life intangibles are not amortized; they're tested for impairment at least annually. Finite-life intangibles (patents, licenses) are amortized. Analysts often remove goodwill when computing ratios such as tangible book value.

3.3 Equity

Contributed capital (common and preferred stock, additional paid-in capital) + retained earnings − treasury stock (bought-back shares, which have no votes or dividends) + accumulated OCI + non-controlling (minority) interest.

Part 4 · Learning modules 4–5

Analyzing statements of cash flows

Classification, the direct and indirect methods, free cash flow and cash flow ratios.

CFO Operating

Cash from day-to-day business: collections from customers, payments to suppliers and employees, taxes, and (US GAAP) interest paid and interest and dividends received.

CFI Investing

Buying and selling long-term assets and investments in other companies' securities; making and collecting loans to others.

CFF Financing

Issuing and repaying debt, issuing and buying back shares, and (US GAAP) dividends paid.

Non-cash transactions (e.g. converting debt to equity, buying an asset with a loan) don't appear in the statement; they're disclosed in the notes.

4.1 Direct vs indirect method

Direct method

Lists actual cash receipts and payments (cash from customers, cash to suppliers…). More informative; encouraged by both IFRS and US GAAP, but rarely used. US GAAP requires a reconciliation (the indirect method) in the notes if the direct method is used.

Indirect method

Starts with net income and adjusts for non-cash items and working capital changes. Most companies use it. Only CFO differs between the two methods; CFI and CFF are the same.

4.2 Free cash flow and ratios

Free cash flow to the firm
FCFF = CFO + Interest × (1 − t) − FCInv
FCInv = capital expenditure (net of disposals). Under IFRS, if interest paid is in CFF, don't add it back.
Free cash flow to equity
FCFE = CFO − FCInv + Net borrowing

Cash flow ratios

Cash flow to revenue (CFO/revenue) · cash return on assets (CFO/avg assets) · cash to income (CFO/operating income) · cash flow per share · debt coverage (CFO/total debt) · interest coverage ((CFO + interest + taxes)/interest) · reinvestment (CFO/capex).
Warning sign. CFO persistently below net income can mean aggressive revenue or expense recognition. Also check whether CFO is boosted by stretching payables or selling receivables.
Part 5 · Learning modules 6–7

Inventories and long-lived assets

Cost flow methods, the LIFO reserve, write-downs, capitalizing, depreciation, impairment and revaluation.

5.1 Inventory cost flow methods

FIFO

First in, first out: the oldest costs go to COGS; ending inventory reflects the most recent costs. Allowed under IFRS and US GAAP.

Weighted average

COGS and inventory both use the average cost of goods available. Allowed under both.

LIFO

Last in, first out: newest costs go to COGS. US GAAP only. Popular in the US because it lowers taxes when prices rise.
FIFO vs weighted average vs LIFO

5.2 The LIFO reserve

Inventory
InvFIFO = InvLIFO + LIFO reserve
COGS
COGSFIFO = COGSLIFO − ΔLIFO reserve
Net income
NIFIFO = NILIFO + ΔLIFO reserve × (1 − t)
  • Converting the balance sheet to FIFO: equity rises by LIFO reserve × (1 − t); liabilities (deferred taxes) rise by LIFO reserve × t.
  • LIFO liquidation: when inventory quantities fall, old low costs flow into COGS, inflating profit for a one-off period. A falling LIFO reserve is the clue. It's unsustainable and a reporting-quality red flag.

5.3 Inventory valuation

IFRS

Lower of cost and net realizable value (estimated selling price − costs to complete and sell). Write-downs can be reversed (up to the original cost).

US GAAP

FIFO and average cost: lower of cost and NRV. LIFO and retail method: lower of cost or market. Write-downs can't be reversed.

Periodic inventory systems update inventory at period end; perpetual systems update with each sale. FIFO and specific identification give the same result under both; LIFO and average cost can differ.

5.4 Capitalizing vs expensing

Capitalize or expense the same spending
Before this spending, net income is $500 a year and CFO is $600. Taxes are ignored. Capitalizing spreads the cost over its life; expensing hits year 1.
Net income if capitalizedNet income if expensed
  • Capitalizing gives smoother, higher early profits, higher assets and equity, and higher CFO (the outflow is in CFI). Profitability ratios are higher in year 1 and lower later (bigger asset base).
  • Interest during construction is capitalized into the asset's cost under both IFRS and US GAAP; it's then in CFI, not CFO.
  • R&D: IFRS expenses research and capitalizes development once specific criteria are met. US GAAP generally expenses both (with exceptions such as some software).

5.6 Impairment, revaluation and derecognition

IFRS impairment

Impaired if carrying amount > recoverable amount = higher of (fair value − costs to sell) and value in use (PV of future cash flows). Loss = carrying amount − recoverable amount. Reversals allowed (not for goodwill).

US GAAP impairment (assets held for use)

Step 1, recoverability: impaired if carrying amount > undiscounted future cash flows. Step 2: loss = carrying amount − fair value. No reversals.
  • Revaluation model (IFRS only): PP&E carried at fair value. Increases go to OCI (revaluation surplus) unless they reverse a past loss in profit; decreases go to profit unless they reverse a past surplus.
  • Investment property (IFRS): cost model or fair value model (fair value changes go to profit).
  • Derecognition: gain or loss on sale = proceeds − carrying amount. Assets held for sale are carried at the lower of carrying amount and fair value less costs to sell, and are no longer depreciated.
  • Impairments are non-cash: they reduce net income and assets but not CFO.
Part 6 · Learning modules 8–9

Long-term liabilities, equity and income taxes

Bonds, leases, pensions, share-based pay, and deferred taxes.

6.1 Bonds: the effective interest method

Interest expense
Carrying value × Market rate at issuance
The coupon is the cash paid. The difference amortizes the discount or premium.

Discount bond

Coupon < market rate. Issued below par. Interest expense > coupon; carrying value rises to par.

Premium bond

Coupon > market rate. Issued above par. Interest expense < coupon; carrying value falls to par.
Carrying value and interest expense
A $1,000, 10-year annual-coupon bond. Interest expense uses the market rate at issuance.
Carrying valuePar
Issue price
Year-1 interest expense
Year-1 coupon paid
  • Under US GAAP the coupon paid is in CFO; issuing and repaying principal is in CFF. Under IFRS, interest paid can be CFO or CFF.
  • Retiring debt early: gain or loss = carrying amount − amount paid. Issue costs reduce the carrying amount (both IFRS and US GAAP).
  • Covenants protect lenders: affirmative (must do) and negative (must not do). Breaching one can make the debt due immediately.

6.2 Leases

Lessee: IFRS 16

One model: almost all leases go on the balance sheet as a right-of-use asset and a lease liability (PV of payments). The income statement shows depreciation plus interest, front-loaded. Short-term (≤ 12 months) and low-value leases can be expensed.

Lessee: US GAAP (ASC 842)

Both types go on the balance sheet. Finance lease: depreciation + interest (like IFRS). Operating lease: a single straight-line lease cost. A finance lease meets any one test: ownership transfers, purchase option reasonably certain, term is a major part of the asset's life, PV of payments ≥ substantially all of fair value, or the asset is specialized.
Lease expense pattern
The same lease, $1,000 a year paid at year-end. A finance-style lease front-loads expense; an operating lease (US GAAP) is level.
Depreciation (finance / IFRS)Interest (finance / IFRS)Operating lease cost (US GAAP)
Initial lease liability and ROU asset
Year-1 expense: finance vs operating

Cash flows: for a finance lease, the principal part of the payment is in CFF (interest in CFO under US GAAP). For a US GAAP operating lease, the whole payment is in CFO. So finance-lease treatment shows higher CFO. Lessors: a finance (IFRS) or sales-type (US GAAP) lease derecognizes the asset and records a receivable; an operating lease keeps the asset and records rental income.

6.3 Pensions and share-based compensation

Defined contribution

Expense = the contribution the company owes for the period. The employee bears the investment risk. No liability beyond unpaid contributions.

Defined benefit

Net pension liability (asset) = PV of the defined benefit obligation − fair value of plan assets. Underfunded = liability. IFRS: service cost and net interest go to profit; remeasurements (actuarial gains and losses, return on assets beyond the interest) go to OCI and stay there. US GAAP: service cost, interest cost and expected return go to profit; actuarial gains and losses go to OCI and are amortized into profit over time.

Share-based pay: stock grants and options are measured at fair value on the grant date and expensed over the vesting (service) period. Options are valued with an option-pricing model, so the expense depends on assumptions such as volatility. Stock appreciation rights are settled in cash and remeasured each period.

6.4 Income taxes

Two sets of books

The financial statements show accounting profit (pretax income) and income tax expense. The tax return shows taxable income and taxes payable. Timing differences between them create deferred taxes.

How a deferred tax liability builds and reverses
A $1,000 asset with a 5-year life. The books use straight-line; the tax return uses double-declining balance (switching to straight-line). The line shows the DTL on the balance sheet at each year-end.
Book depreciationTax depreciationDTL balance
  • Permanent differences (e.g. tax-exempt interest, non-deductible fines) never reverse: no deferred tax, but they make the effective tax rate (tax expense ÷ pretax income) differ from the statutory rate.
  • Valuation allowance (US GAAP): reduces a DTA when it's more likely than not that it won't be used. It raises tax expense and lowers net income. IFRS recognizes a DTA only if it's probable it will be used.
  • A change in tax rates remeasures DTLs and DTAs, and the change flows through tax expense.
  • Analyst view of a DTL: if it's not expected to reverse (e.g. a growing company keeps buying assets), treat it as equity; if it will reverse, treat it as a liability.
Part 7 · Learning modules 10–12

Reporting quality, analysis techniques and modeling

How much to trust the numbers, how to analyze them, and how to forecast.

7.1 Financial reporting quality

Spectrum of reporting quality
From best (top) to worst (bottom).
GAAP, decision-useful, sustainable earnings with adequate returns
GAAP, decision-useful, but low-quality (unsustainable) earnings
Within GAAP, but biased accounting choices
Within GAAP, but "earnings management" through real actions
Departures from GAAP: non-compliant accounting
Departures from GAAP: fictitious transactions (fraud)

Key ideas

  • Reporting quality = how useful and faithful the reports are. Earnings quality = how sustainable the earnings are. You can have high-quality reporting of low-quality earnings.
  • Conservative choices understate current results; aggressive ones overstate them. Both are biased. Conservative accounting can also enable "cookie jar" reserves to release later.
  • Why it happens (the fraud triangle): motivation (bonuses, meeting forecasts, covenants), opportunity (weak controls, flexible rules), rationalization.
  • Checks: auditors, regulators, market discipline, private contracts (covenants).
  • Non-GAAP measures must be reconciled to the closest GAAP figure; watch for items excluded every year.
Warning signs. CFO persistently below net income · receivables or inventory growing faster than sales · aggressive revenue recognition (bill-and-hold, channel stuffing) · capitalizing costs others expense · longer useful lives or lower bad-debt estimates than peers · "non-recurring" charges every year · LIFO liquidations · big bath charges · related-party transactions · frequent auditor changes · results that just meet forecasts every quarter.

7.3 Financial statement modeling

Revenue

Top-down: grow with GDP or the market and apply a market share. Bottom-up: volume × price by product, segment or store. Hybrid approaches combine both.

Costs and capital

COGS often as a % of revenue (watch input prices and hedging); SG&A has fixed and variable parts. Working capital from days ratios; capex split between maintenance (≈ depreciation) and growth.

Judgment

Scenario and sensitivity analysis; competitive position (Porter's five forces); inflation and deflation pass-through to prices; technology disruption; choosing the forecast horizon.

Behavioral biases in forecasting: overconfidence (ranges too narrow), illusion of control (more detail ≠ more accuracy), conservatism (slow to update), representativeness (over-weighting recent or similar cases), confirmation bias (seeking only supporting evidence).

8Net income 100, depreciation 20, receivables up 15, inventory down 10, payables down 5, gain on sale of equipment 8. CFO is: A. 92 B. 102 C. 118Show answer
B. 100 + 20 − 15 + 10 − 5 − 8 = 102. C adds the gain instead of subtracting it.
9CFO 500, interest paid 50 (in CFO), tax rate 30%, capital expenditure 200. FCFF is: A. 300 B. 335 C. 350Show answer
B. 500 + 50 × 0.7 − 200 = 335. C adds back interest before tax.
10With rising prices and stable inventory, compared with FIFO, LIFO gives: A. higher gross margin B. lower taxes paid C. higher ending inventoryShow answer
B. Higher COGS → lower taxable income → lower taxes and higher CFO.
11LIFO inventory is $400 and the LIFO reserve is $100. FIFO inventory is: A. $300 B. $400 C. $500Show answer
C. LIFO inventory + LIFO reserve.
12LIFO COGS is $1,000 and the LIFO reserve rose from $80 to $100. FIFO COGS is: A. $980 B. $1,000 C. $1,020Show answer
A. 1,000 − 20. Rising prices mean FIFO COGS is lower.
13Reversing a prior inventory write-down is: A. allowed under IFRS only B. allowed under US GAAP only C. allowed under bothShow answer
A.
14A falling LIFO reserve combined with a sharp rise in gross margin most likely signals: A. a LIFO liquidation B. a switch to FIFO C. falling salesShow answer
A. Old, cheap layers flowed into COGS, boosting profit for a one-off period.
15Cost $10,000, salvage $1,000, 5-year life. Double-declining depreciation in year 2 is: A. $1,800 B. $2,160 C. $2,400Show answer
C. Year 1: 40% × 10,000 = 4,000. Year 2: 40% × 6,000 = 2,400. A is straight-line. B wrongly subtracts salvage before applying the rate.
16Compared with expensing, capitalizing an expenditure in year 1 gives: A. higher CFO B. higher CFI C. lower total assetsShow answer
A. The outflow moves to CFI (lower CFI), so CFO is higher; assets are higher.
17Under IFRS, carrying amount 1,000; fair value less costs to sell 880; value in use 920. The impairment loss is: A. 0 B. 80 C. 120Show answer
B. Recoverable amount = higher of 880 and 920 = 920. Loss = 1,000 − 920.
18Under US GAAP, carrying amount 1,000; undiscounted future cash flows 1,100; fair value 900. The impairment loss is: A. 0 B. 100 C. 200Show answer
A. Carrying amount is below undiscounted cash flows, so it passes the recoverability test.
19Accumulated depreciation 400, annual depreciation 100, gross PP&E 1,000. Estimated remaining useful life is: A. 4 years B. 6 years C. 10 yearsShow answer
B. Net PP&E 600 / 100. A is the average age; C the total life.
20A $1,000, 5-year, 5% annual bond is issued when market rates are 6%, at $957.88. Year-1 interest expense is closest to: A. $50.00 B. $57.47 C. $60.00Show answer
B. 957.88 × 6%. A is the coupon; C uses par.
21Under IFRS 16, a lessee's total lease-related expense over the lease term is typically: A. level each year B. higher in early years C. higher in later yearsShow answer
B. Depreciation is level but interest is highest early, when the liability is largest.
22PV of the defined benefit obligation is 1,200 and plan assets are 1,000. The balance sheet shows a: A. net pension asset of 200 B. net pension liability of 200 C. pension liability of 1,200Show answer
B. The plan is underfunded by 200.
23An asset costs 300 with a 3-year life. Book depreciation is straight-line (100); tax depreciation in year 1 is 150. At a 30% tax rate, the DTL at the end of year 1 is: A. 15 B. 30 C. 45Show answer
A. Carrying amount 200 − tax base 150 = 50 × 30% = 15.
24Taxes payable are 200, the DTL rose by 15 and the DTA rose by 5. Income tax expense is: A. 190 B. 210 C. 220Show answer
B. 200 + 15 − 5.
25Increasing a valuation allowance on deferred tax assets will: A. raise net income B. lower net income C. have no effect on net incomeShow answer
B. It reduces the DTA and raises tax expense.
26Net profit margin 5%, asset turnover 1.6, financial leverage 2.5. ROE is: A. 8% B. 12.5% C. 20%Show answer
C. 0.05 × 1.6 × 2.5 = 0.20. A is ROA.
27DOH 60 days, DSO 45 days, days of payables 30. The cash conversion cycle is: A. 75 days B. 105 days C. 135 daysShow answer
A. 60 + 45 − 30.
28Which most likely indicates low earnings quality? A. CFO consistently above net income B. Receivables growing much faster than revenue C. Stable gross marginsShow answer
B. It can signal aggressive revenue recognition or weak collections.
