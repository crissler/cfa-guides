Part 1 · Learning modules 1–2

Rates, returns and time value of money

What an interest rate is made of, how to measure returns, and how to move money through time.

1.1 Interest rates

Building blocks of an interest rate
r = Real risk-free rate + Inflation premium + Default risk premium + Liquidity premium + Maturity premium
Nominal risk-free rate ≈ real risk-free rate + inflation premium (e.g. a T-bill).

An interest rate can be read three ways: the required return investors demand, the discount rate used to find present values, and the opportunity cost of spending money now instead of investing it.

1.2 Return measures

Holding period return
R = P1 − P0 + D1P0
Arithmetic mean
R̄ = ΣRtT
Best estimate of a single future period's return.
Geometric mean
[(1+R1)(1+R2)…(1+RT)]1/T − 1
Compound growth rate. Best for past multi-period performance.
Harmonic mean
X̄H = nΣ(1/Xi)
Average cost per share when you invest the same dollar amount each period (cost averaging).
Money-weighted return
The IRR of the investor's cash flows
Affected by when money goes in and out.
Time-weighted return
[(1+R1)…(1+Rn)]1/years − 1
Ignores cash-flow timing. Standard for judging managers.
Ordering rule. Unless every return is the same: arithmetic mean > geometric mean > harmonic mean. The gap widens as returns get more volatile. A useful approximation: geometric ≈ arithmetic − σ²/2.
  • Trimmed mean: drop a set % of the highest and lowest values, then average. Winsorized mean: replace the extremes with the nearest remaining values. Both reduce the effect of outliers.
  • Other returns: gross (before fees) vs net (after fees); pre-tax vs after-tax; real = (1 + nominal)/(1 + inflation) − 1; leveraged return = Rp + (VB/VE)(Rp − rD).
  • Annualizing: (1 + Rperiod)periods per year − 1.
Volatility drag: why the geometric mean is lower
Returns alternate between average + volatility and average − volatility. The arithmetic mean stays the same; your actual wealth grows at the geometric mean.

1.3 Compounding

Effective annual rate
EAR = (1 + Stated ratem)m − 1
m = compounding periods per year.
Continuous compounding
EAR = er − 1  ·  FV = PV × erT
Continuously compounded return
r = ln(1 + HPR) = ln(P1/P0)
These add up over time: r0,T = r1 + r2 + … + rT.
EAR calculator
More frequent compounding raises the effective rate, up to a limit set by continuous compounding.

1.4 Time value of money

Future and present value
FV = PV(1 + r)N  ·  PV = FV(1 + r)N
Ordinary annuity (payments at period end)
PV = PMT × 1 − (1 + r)−Nr
Annuity due (payments at period start)
PVdue = PVordinary × (1 + r)
Each payment arrives one period sooner.
Perpetuity
PV = PMTr
e.g. preferred stock: dividend / required return.
Growing perpetuity (Gordon growth)
PV0 = D1r − g
Requires r > g. Uses next year's dividend.
Implied return and growth
r = D1P0 + g  ·  g = r − D1P0
  • Annuity due vs ordinary: rent and insurance premiums are paid at the start (annuity due); bond coupons and loan payments at the end (ordinary).
  • Uneven cash flows: discount each one separately and add them up, or use the CF worksheet for NPV.
  • Multistage growth: discount the high-growth dividends one by one, then add a Gordon-model terminal value discounted from the year it applies.
  • Cash flow additivity: cash flows at the same date can be added. Two investments with identical cash flows must have the same price (no arbitrage). This is the basis for forward rates, e.g. investing 2 years at z2 = investing 1 year at z1, then 1 year at the 1y1y forward.
Part 2 · Learning module 3

Statistical measures of asset returns

Center, spread, shape and co-movement.

2.1 Central tendency and location

Mean, median, mode

Median = middle value (average the two middle values if n is even); not affected by outliers. Mode = most frequent value; a distribution can have more than one.

Quantile position
Ly = (n + 1) × y100
Position of the y-th percentile in sorted data. Interpolate if it falls between two values.

Quantiles and box plots

Quartiles (4 parts), quintiles (5), deciles (10), percentiles (100). Interquartile range = Q3 − Q1. A box plot shows the median, the IQR box, and whiskers to the extremes.

2.2 Dispersion

Mean absolute deviation
MAD = Σ|Xi − X̄|n
Sample variance
s² = Σ(Xi − X̄)²n − 1
Divide by n − 1 for a sample. Standard deviation s = √s².
Target downside deviation
sTarget = ΣXi < B(Xi − B)²n − 1
Only counts returns below the target B. Measures downside risk.
Coefficient of variation
CV = sX̄
Risk per unit of return. Lower is better. Lets you compare series with different averages.

2.3 Shape: skewness and kurtosis

Skewness moves the mean, median and mode apart
Drag to skew the distribution. The mean is pulled furthest toward the long tail.
MeanMedianMode

Skewness

Positive (right) long right tail: mean > median > mode. Many small losses, a few large gains.
Negative (left) long left tail: mean < median < mode. Many small gains, a few large losses. Common for stock returns, and bad for investors.

Kurtosis

Normal distribution kurtosis = 3, excess kurtosis = 0 (mesokurtic).
Leptokurtic excess kurtosis > 0: fatter tails and a taller peak. Extreme returns are more common than the normal distribution predicts.
Platykurtic excess kurtosis < 0: thinner tails.
Fat tails (leptokurtic) vs normal
Both distributions have the same mean and standard deviation.
Normal (excess kurtosis 0)Leptokurtic (excess kurtosis 3)

2.4 Covariance and correlation

Sample covariance
sXY = Σ(Xi − X̄)(Yi − Ȳ)n − 1
Correlation
rXY = sXYsX sY
Always between −1 and +1. No units.
What correlation looks like

Limits of correlation. It only measures linear relationships (a perfect curve can show r ≈ 0). Outliers can distort it. Spurious correlation: two series can be correlated by chance, or because a third variable drives both. Correlation is not causation.
Part 3 · Learning modules 4–5

Probability, expected value and portfolio math

Probability rules, trees, Bayes' formula, covariance from probabilities, and safety-first.

3.1 Probability rules

Multiplication rule (joint probability)
P(AB) = P(A | B) × P(B)
If A and B are independent: P(AB) = P(A) × P(B).
Addition rule
P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(AB)
If mutually exclusive, P(AB) = 0.
Total probability rule
P(A) = Σ P(A | Si) × P(Si)
Si = mutually exclusive, exhaustive scenarios.
Odds
Odds for E = P(E)1 − P(E)
Odds of 1 to 3 → P = 1/4.

Independent events: one doesn't change the other's probability, P(A | B) = P(A). Mutually exclusive events can't both happen. Don't confuse the two: mutually exclusive events are actually very dependent.

3.2 Expected value, variance and trees

Expected value
E(X) = Σ P(xi) × xi
Variance from probabilities
σ² = Σ P(xi) × [xi − E(X)]²
No n − 1 here: probabilities are the weights.
Conditional expectation
E(X) = Σ E(X | Si) × P(Si)
A probability tree: multiply along the branches, add across the ends.

3.3 Bayes' formula

Updating a probability with new information
P(Event | Info) = P(Info | Event)P(Info) × P(Event)
P(Info) comes from the total probability rule: P(Info | Event)P(Event) + P(Info | not Event)P(not Event).
Bayes as areas
Each column is a group of the population. The shaded parts got the signal. The updated probability is the shaded share that belongs to the event.

3.4 Portfolio mathematics

Portfolio expected return
E(Rp) = Σ wi E(Ri)
Two-asset variance
σp² = w1²σ1² + w2²σ2² + 2w1w2Cov1,2
Cov1,2 = ρ1,2σ1σ2. Three assets: three variance terms plus three covariance terms.
Covariance from a joint probability table
Cov(RA, RB) = Σ P(RA, RB) [RA − E(RA)][RB − E(RB)]
Roy's safety-first ratio
SFRatio = E(Rp) − RLσp
RL = minimum acceptable (threshold) return. Choose the portfolio with the highest ratio: it has the lowest chance of falling below RL.

Shortfall probability

If returns are normal, P(Rp < RL) = N(−SFRatio). With RL = risk-free rate, the SFRatio is the Sharpe ratio.
Safety-first: which portfolio wins?
The steepest line from the threshold return marks the best portfolio. Change the threshold and the winner can change.
Part 4 · Learning module 6 and background

Distributions and simulation

The normal and lognormal distributions, Monte Carlo simulation and bootstrapping.

4.1 The normal distribution

Key facts

  • Symmetric: mean = median = mode
  • Fully described by mean and variance
  • Skewness 0, kurtosis 3
  • A linear combination of normal variables is normal

How much falls within…

  • ±1σ: about 68%
  • ±1.65σ: 90%
  • ±1.96σ: 95%
  • ±2.58σ: 99%
Standardizing (z-score)
z = X − μσ
Number of standard deviations from the mean.

4.2 The lognormal distribution

If ln(X) is normal, X is lognormal. It's bounded below by zero and skewed to the right, which makes it a good model for asset prices (a price can't go below zero). If continuously compounded returns are normal, prices are lognormal.
Lognormal mean and median
Mean = eμ + σ²/2  ·  Median = eμ
μ and σ are the mean and standard deviation of ln(X). Mean > median (right skew).
Scaling volatility over time (independent returns)
σT = σ × √T
e.g. annual volatility ≈ daily volatility × √250. Returns add up over time; volatility grows with the square root.

Monte Carlo: uses and limits

Used for complex securities, pension planning and risk (VaR). Limits: results are only as good as the assumed distributions (garbage in, garbage out); it gives numbers, not an explanation of cause and effect.

Historical simulation and bootstrapping

Historical simulation reuses actual past outcomes. Bootstrapping draws repeatedly with replacement from the observed sample to build many "new" samples, then studies the statistic across them. It needs no distribution assumption, but it assumes the sample represents the population.
Part 5 · Learning modules 7–9

Estimation, inference and hypothesis testing

Sampling, the central limit theorem, confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, and tests of independence.

5.1 Sampling

Probability sampling

  • Simple random: every member equally likely
  • Stratified random: split into groups (strata), sample from each in proportion. Used to build bond index portfolios.
  • Cluster: split into clusters, sample whole clusters (one-stage) or sample within chosen clusters (two-stage). Cheaper, less accurate.

Non-probability sampling

  • Convenience: whatever is easy to get
  • Judgmental: an expert picks the sample

Faster and cheaper, but may not represent the population.

Sampling error and biases

Sampling error = sample statistic − population parameter. Biases: data snooping (testing many patterns until one "works"), survivorship (only surviving funds or firms), look-ahead (using data not yet available at the time), time-period (results specific to one period).

5.2 The central limit theorem and standard error

For a large enough sample (n ≥ 30), the sample mean is approximately normal, whatever the shape of the population, with mean μ and variance σ²/n.
Standard error of the sample mean
σX̄ = σ√n  (or s√n if σ is unknown)
Bigger samples → smaller standard error → more precise estimates.
The central limit theorem in action
The population is heavily skewed (exponential, mean 1). Each bar chart shows 3,000 sample means. As n grows, they pile up into a narrow normal curve.

5.3 Point estimates and confidence intervals

Good estimators are…

Unbiased: expected value equals the parameter.
Efficient: smallest variance among unbiased estimators.
Consistent: more accurate as n grows.
Confidence interval
X̄ ± Reliability factor × s√n
Point estimate ± critical value × standard error.

Which reliability factor?

z: normal population with known σ, or any large sample.
t: σ unknown (t is fine for large samples too). t has fatter tails than z, approaching z as degrees of freedom grow.
Small sample from a non-normal population: no reliable interval.

Parametric vs non-parametric

Parametric tests make assumptions about the distribution (e.g. normal) and test parameters like the mean. Non-parametric tests use ranks or counts. Use them when assumptions fail, data have outliers, data are ranks, or the question isn't about a parameter. Example: Spearman rank correlation.

Multiple testing

Run enough tests and some will look significant by chance (false discoveries). The false discovery rate approach (Benjamini–Hochberg) adjusts the p-value cutoffs. Also separate statistical significance from economic significance: a tiny effect can be "significant" yet unprofitable after costs.
Part 6 · Learning module 10

Simple linear regression

Fitting a line, judging the fit, testing the slope, and predicting.

6.1 The model

Regression line
Yi = b0 + b1Xi + εi
Y = dependent (explained) variable; X = independent (explanatory) variable.
Slope
b1 = Cov(X, Y)Var(X)
Intercept
b0 = Ȳ − b1X̄
The line always passes through (X̄, Ȳ).

Ordinary least squares chooses the line that minimizes the sum of squared residuals (SSE). Residual = actual Y − predicted Y.

Four assumptions: (1) Linearity between X and Y. (2) Homoskedasticity: residuals have constant variance. (3) Independence: residuals aren't correlated with each other. (4) Normality: residuals are normally distributed. Residual plots reveal violations, such as a fan shape (heteroskedasticity) or a pattern over time.

6.2 Goodness of fit: ANOVA

Sum of squares
SST = SSR + SSE
Total = explained (regression) + unexplained (error).
Coefficient of determination
R² = SSRSST
% of Y's variation explained by X. In simple regression, R² = r².
Standard error of the estimate
SEE = √MSE = SSEn − 2
Typical size of a residual. Smaller = better fit.
F-statistic
F = MSRMSE = SSR / 1SSE / (n − 2)
Tests whether the slope is zero. In simple regression, F = t² of the slope.
t-test of the slope
t = b1 − B1sb1
df = n − 2. Usually B1 = 0. Same structure for the intercept.

Predicted value

6.4 Functional forms and dummy variables

ModelEquationSlope means…
Log-linln Y = b0 + b1XA 1-unit change in X changes Y by about b1 × 100%. Fits constant growth over time.
Lin-logY = b0 + b1 ln XA 1% change in X changes Y by about b1/100 units.
Log-logln Y = b0 + b1 ln XA 1% change in X changes Y by b1% (elasticity).
Indicator (dummy)Y = b0 + b1D, with D = 0 or 1b0 = mean of Y when D = 0; b1 = difference in means between the groups.
Traps. The sign of the correlation equals the sign of the slope, but R² is always positive: r = ±√R². Don't use a regression to predict far outside the X values in the data. A significant slope doesn't prove X causes Y.
Part 7 · Learning module 11

Introduction to big data techniques

Big data, alternative data, machine learning and its pitfalls.

Characteristics of big data

  • Volume: huge quantity
  • Velocity: arrives fast, often real time
  • Variety: structured (tables), semi-structured, unstructured (text, images, video)
  • Veracity: can the data be trusted?

Sources of alternative data

  • Individuals: social media, reviews, web searches
  • Business processes: transactions, credit card data, corporate records
  • Sensors: satellites, geolocation, the Internet of Things

Working with the data

Capture → curation (cleaning errors) → storage → search → transfer. Visualize with tag (word) clouds and mind maps. Natural language processing turns text (earnings calls, filings, news) into signals.

Overfitting vs underfitting
As a model gets more complex, it fits the training data ever better. Error on new data falls at first, then rises once the model starts fitting noise.
Error on training data (in-sample)Error on new data (out-of-sample)

Overfitting

Model is too complex and learns the noise. Great in-sample, poor out-of-sample. Fix: simpler model, more data, cross-validation.

Training, validation and test samples

Train the model on one set, tune it on a validation set, and judge it on a test set it has never seen.

9A distribution with excess kurtosis of 2 is: A. platykurtic B. mesokurtic C. leptokurticShow answer
C. Positive excess kurtosis = fatter tails than normal.
10Fund X: mean 8%, standard deviation 12%. Fund Y: mean 5%, standard deviation 6%. Which has less risk per unit of return? A. X B. Y C. They're equalShow answer
B. CVX = 12/8 = 1.5; CVY = 6/5 = 1.2. Lower CV is better.
11Scenarios: 30% chance of 20%, 50% chance of 10%, 20% chance of −10%. The expected return is: A. 6.7% B. 9.0% C. 10.0%Show answer
B. 0.3(20) + 0.5(10) + 0.2(−10) = 6 + 5 − 2 = 9%. A is the simple average of the three returns.
1240% of managers outperform. 80% of outperformers get a positive signal, as do 30% of underperformers. Given a positive signal, the probability a manager outperforms is: A. 40% B. 64% C. 80%Show answer
B. P(signal) = 0.8 × 0.4 + 0.3 × 0.6 = 0.32 + 0.18 = 0.50. Posterior = 0.32/0.50 = 64%. C confuses P(signal | outperform) with P(outperform | signal).
13Threshold return 3%. A: E(R) 9%, σ 12%. B: 7%, 6%. C: 12%, 20%. By Roy's safety-first criterion, choose: A. A B. B C. CShow answer
B. SFR: A = 6/12 = 0.50; B = 4/6 = 0.67; C = 9/20 = 0.45. Highest ratio wins.
14Population σ is 20% and the sample size is 100. The standard error of the sample mean is: A. 0.2% B. 2% C. 20%Show answer
B. 20% / √100 = 2%.
15Sample mean 10%, s = 15%, n = 36. The 95% confidence interval is closest to: A. 5.1% to 14.9% B. 7.5% to 12.5% C. −19.4% to 39.4%Show answer
A. 10 ± 1.96 × 15/√36 = 10 ± 1.96 × 2.5 = 10 ± 4.9. C forgets to divide by √n.
16A Type I error is: A. rejecting a true null B. failing to reject a false null C. rejecting a false nullShow answer
A. Its probability is α, the significance level. B is a Type II error.
17A test's p-value is 0.03. At the 1% significance level you should: A. reject H0 B. fail to reject H0 C. accept Ha automaticallyShow answer
B. Reject only if p < α. 0.03 > 0.01. (At 5% you would reject.)
18Testing H0: μ = 0 with X̄ = 1.5%, s = 6%, n = 36. The t-statistic is: A. 0.25 B. 1.50 C. 9.00Show answer
B. t = 1.5 / (6/√36) = 1.5 / 1 = 1.50. A forgets √n.
19To test whether two populations have equal variances, use the: A. t-test B. chi-square test C. F-testShow answer
C. F = s1²/s2². Chi-square tests a single variance.
20Comparing returns of the same stocks before and after an event calls for a: A. paired comparisons test B. difference in means test C. F-testShow answer
A. The samples are dependent (same stocks), so test the mean of the differences.
21Sample correlation 0.30 with n = 38. The t-statistic for testing zero correlation is closest to: A. 0.30 B. 1.80 C. 1.89Show answer
C. 0.30 × √36 / √(1 − 0.09) = 1.80 / 0.954 = 1.89. B forgets the denominator.
22A chi-square test of independence on a table with 3 rows and 4 columns has degrees of freedom: A. 6 B. 7 C. 12Show answer
A. (3 − 1)(4 − 1) = 6.
23Cov(X, Y) = 0.0036, Var(X) = 0.0045, mean X = 4%, mean Y = 5%. The intercept is closest to: A. 0.8% B. 1.8% C. 5.0%Show answer
B. Slope = 0.0036/0.0045 = 0.8. Intercept = 5% − 0.8 × 4% = 1.8%.
24A regression with n = 22 has SST = 100 and SSE = 40. The F-statistic is: A. 1.5 B. 20 C. 30Show answer
C. SSR = 60; MSR = 60/1; MSE = 40/(22 − 2) = 2; F = 60/2 = 30. R² = 0.60, SEE = √2 = 1.41.
25Which model best fits a variable that grows at a constant rate over time? A. Log-lin B. Lin-log C. Log-logShow answer
A. ln Y = b0 + b1 × time; b1 ≈ the growth rate.
26Grouping companies by similarity without any labeled outcomes is an example of: A. supervised learning B. unsupervised learning C. reinforcement learningShow answer
B. Clustering with no labels = unsupervised.
27A model fits its training data very well but predicts poorly on new data. It is most likely: A. underfit B. overfit C. well specifiedShow answer
B. It learned the noise in the training data.
28The lognormal distribution is commonly used for asset prices because it: A. is symmetric B. is bounded below by zero C. has thin tailsShow answer
B. Prices can't be negative; the lognormal is also right-skewed.
