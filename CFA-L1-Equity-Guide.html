Part 1 · Learning module 1

Market organization and structure

What the financial system does

Lets people save, borrow, raise equity, manage risk, exchange assets now and in the future, and use information. It allocates capital to its most productive uses and sets prices that balance supply and demand.

Intermediaries

Brokers and exchanges (connect traders); dealers (trade from their own inventory); securitizers; depository institutions; insurers; arbitrageurs; clearinghouses and custodians (settle trades, hold assets safely).

Positions

Long: own the asset, gain if it rises. Short: sell a borrowed asset, gain if it falls; the short seller pays any dividends to the lender and faces unlimited potential loss. Leveraged: buy partly with borrowed money (on margin).

1.2 Orders

InstructionExamples
Execution (how to fill)Market order: fill now at the best price (certain execution, uncertain price). Limit order: only at the limit price or better (price control, may not fill). All-or-nothing; hidden orders; iceberg orders (only part displayed).
Validity (when it's active)Day order; good-till-cancelled; immediate-or-cancel (fill-or-kill); good-on-close/open; stop orders (become active only after the price hits a stop price; a stop-loss sell protects a long position)
Clearing (how to settle)Who clears and settles the trade; for sells, whether it's a long sale or a short sale

A limit buy below the market price is "behind the market"; at the best bid it "makes a new market" only if it improves the best bid; a limit buy at or above the best offer is "marketable" and fills immediately.

1.3 Primary and secondary markets

Primary markets

Issuers sell new securities: IPOs and seasoned offerings (underwritten, best efforts, auctions), shelf registrations, rights offerings (existing holders can buy new shares at a discount), private placements.

Secondary markets

Investors trade with each other. Liquid secondary markets lower issuers' cost of capital by making primary issues more attractive. Call markets trade at set times; continuous markets trade throughout the day.

Execution mechanisms

Quote-driven (dealer) markets; order-driven markets (orders matched by rules: price, display, time priority); brokered markets (for unique assets, e.g. real estate).

A well-functioning financial system has…

Complete markets (people can save, borrow, hedge); operational efficiency (low trading costs); informational efficiency (prices reflect information); allocational efficiency (capital goes to its best uses).

Objectives of regulation

Control fraud; control agency problems; promote fairness; set mutually beneficial standards; prevent undercapitalized firms from exploiting investors; ensure long-term liabilities are funded.
Part 2 · Learning module 2

Security market indexes

WeightingWeight of each stockNotes
Price-weightedPrice ÷ sum of pricesIndex = sum of prices ÷ divisor (adjusted for splits). High-priced stocks dominate. Examples: Dow Jones Industrial Average, Nikkei 225.
Equal-weighted1 ÷ number of stocksOver-weights small companies; must be rebalanced often.
Market-cap weightedMarket cap ÷ total market capLargest companies dominate; over-weights overvalued stocks; self-rebalancing. Float-adjusted uses only shares available to the public.
Fundamental-weightedBased on earnings, sales, dividends or book valueAvoids over-weighting overvalued stocks; tilts toward value.
Same stocks, three indexes
Stock A: $100 × 1,000 shares. Stock B: $50 × 2,000. Stock C: $10 × 10,000. All three have the same market cap ($100,000). Move one price and compare the index returns.

Price return vs total return

Price return uses only price changes. Total return also includes dividends (and other income). Over long periods, total return indexes grow much faster.

Rebalancing vs reconstitution

Rebalancing: restoring target weights (needed for equal weighting). Reconstitution: changing which stocks are in the index.

Uses of indexes

Gauging market sentiment; proxies for the market (e.g. for beta); benchmarks for managers; model portfolios for index funds and ETFs.

Other index types: broad market, multi-market, sector, style (value, growth, size). Fixed-income indexes are hard to build (huge universe, dealer markets, constant turnover as bonds mature). Commodity indexes depend on futures; real estate indexes use appraisals, repeat sales or REITs; hedge fund indexes suffer from survivorship and self-reporting bias.

Part 3 · Learning module 3

Market efficiency

In an efficient market, prices quickly and fully reflect available information, so investors can't consistently earn abnormal (risk-adjusted) returns. Efficiency improves with more participants and analysts, more information, fewer trading restrictions, and lower trading and information costs.

Three forms of efficiency
Each form includes the ones inside it.
If markets are…This can't earn abnormal returns
Weak-form efficientTechnical analysis (past prices and volume)
Semi-strong-form efficientTechnical analysis and fundamental analysis of public information
Strong-form efficientAnything, including insider information

Anomalies

Time-series: calendar effects (January effect, turn-of-the-month), momentum and overreaction. Cross-sectional: size effect, value effect. Other: closed-end fund discounts, slow reaction to earnings surprises, IPO underperformance, predictability from dividend yields. Many disappear once discovered or after transaction costs.

Behavioral finance

Loss aversion, herding, overconfidence, information cascades and other biases can explain some anomalies. Biases don't automatically make markets inefficient; what matters is whether they create exploitable mispricing.
Implications. In efficient markets, passive investing makes sense, and active managers must justify their fees. Portfolio managers still add value by matching portfolios to clients' risk, tax and liquidity needs.
Part 4 · Learning module 4

Overview of equity securities

Common shares

Residual claim; voting rights. Statutory voting: one vote per share per director. Cumulative voting: votes can be concentrated on one candidate, helping minority holders win seats. Callable common shares (company can buy back at a set price) and putable common shares (holder can sell back) also exist.

Preference (preferred) shares

Fixed dividends paid before common; priority in liquidation; usually no votes. Cumulative: missed dividends accrue and must be paid before common dividends. Participating: extra dividends if profits are high. Convertible: can be swapped for common shares.

Risk ranking (lowest to highest): putable preferred < cumulative preferred < non-cumulative preferred < common shares. Callable shares are riskier for investors than otherwise identical non-callable shares; putable shares are less risky.

4.1 Private vs public equity and foreign investing

Private equity

Venture capital, leveraged buyouts (MBOs), private investment in public equity (PIPE). Less liquid, less disclosure, long horizons, managers focus on the long term rather than quarterly results.

Depository receipts

Shares held by a bank that issues receipts in another market. Sponsored (company involved; holders get voting rights) vs unsponsored. ADRs: Level I (over-the-counter), Level II (listed, no new capital), Level III (listed and raises capital), Rule 144A (private placement to institutions). GDRs trade outside the US and the issuer's home market.

Other forms

Direct investing in foreign markets (currency, local rules). Global registered shares trade in different currencies on different exchanges. Basket of listed depository receipts: an ETF holding DRs.

4.2 Risk, return and value

Return sources

Price change + dividends (+ currency gains for foreign shares). Reinvesting dividends compounds returns over time.

Book vs market value

Book value = equity on the balance sheet (historical, accounting-based). Market value = share price × shares, reflecting expected future cash flows. Market-to-book > 1 is normal for growing companies.
Return on equity
ROE = Net incomeAverage book equity
Can rise from more leverage or buybacks, not just better operations.

The company's cost of equity is the minimum return investors require to hold its shares; it equals investors' required return. It isn't observable and must be estimated (e.g. with the DDM or CAPM).

Part 5 · Learning module 5

Company analysis: past and present

Business model

Who are the customers, what's sold, how it's delivered, how it's priced; what makes it profitable.

Revenue drivers

Volume × price; by segment, product and geography; pricing power and market share.

Operating profitability

Gross and operating margins; fixed vs variable costs; operating leverage.

Working capital

Days of inventory, receivables and payables; cash conversion.

Capital investment and structure

Capex, ROIC vs cost of capital, leverage and liquidity.

Operating leverage

The share of costs that are fixed. High operating leverage means a small change in revenue causes a big change in operating profit, in both directions.

Good company research covers…

The investment thesis; the business model and key drivers; past results explained by those drivers; risks; valuation and recommendation. Explain why the numbers changed, not just that they did.
Part 6 · Learning module 6

Industry and competitive analysis

Classifying industries

By products and services, using commercial systems (e.g. GICS, ICB) or government systems. By sensitivity to the business cycle: cyclical (autos, travel) vs non-cyclical (defensive, e.g. utilities and staples, or growth). Peer groups are companies with similar business activities and drivers.

Industry size, growth and profitability

Market size and growth rate; profitability (margins, ROIC) and how it varies across firms; market share trends; concentration (e.g. Herfindahl–Hirschman index).

External influences (PESTLE)

Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Legal and Environmental factors that affect demand, costs and competition.
Industry life cycle
Industry demand over time. Growth slows, competition shifts from winning new customers to taking share.

6.1 Porter's five forces

Threat of new entrants

Low when barriers are high: economies of scale, capital needs, brand loyalty, regulation, distribution access.

Threat of substitutes

Different products that meet the same need cap prices.

Bargaining power of buyers

High when buyers are few, large, or can switch easily.

Bargaining power of suppliers

High when suppliers are few or inputs are unique.

Rivalry among competitors

High with many similar firms, slow growth, high fixed costs, undifferentiated products, high exit barriers.

Competitive strategies: cost leadership (lowest cost), differentiation (unique product, premium price), and focus (a narrow segment, using either). Weak forces mean higher potential profitability for the industry.

Part 7 · Learning module 7

Company analysis: forecasting

What to forecast

Forecast the drivers (volume, price, margins, days ratios) and let the line items follow, rather than forecasting each line separately.

Approaches

Historical results; historical base rates and convergence (e.g. growth and margins drift toward industry averages over time); management guidance; the analyst's own discretionary forecast.

Revenue

Top-down: market size or GDP growth × market share. Bottom-up: volume × price, by segment or store. Hybrid: combine both.

Costs and capital

COGS often as % of revenue; SG&A split into fixed and variable; capex split into maintenance (≈ depreciation) and growth; working capital via days ratios; capital structure via target leverage.

Forecast horizon

Long enough to capture the investment thesis and reach a "normal" state, often through a full business cycle; then a terminal value.

Scenarios and sensitivity

Model base, bull and bear cases and test the key assumptions; identify which drivers matter most to value.
Part 8 · Learning module 8

Equity valuation: concepts and basic tools

Intrinsic value vs price

Intrinsic value > price → undervalued (buy). Intrinsic value < price → overvalued (sell). Close → fairly valued. Before acting, ask how confident you are in the estimate and whether the market might know something you don't.

Three families of models

Present value (discounted cash flow): DDM, FCFE. Multiplier (market multiples): P/E, P/B, P/S, P/CF, EV/EBITDA. Asset-based: market value of assets − liabilities (useful for natural resource firms, financial firms, liquidations).
Gordon (constant) growth DDM
V0 = D1r − g = D0(1 + g)r − g
Requires r > g. Best for mature, stable dividend payers.
Preferred stock (no maturity)
V0 = Dr

g = b × ROE
b = retention ratio = 1 − payout.

CFO − FCInv + Net borrowing
Value = PV of FCFE at the cost of equity. Useful when dividends don't reflect capacity to pay.
Justified forward P/E
P0E1 = D1/E1r − g
Higher payout or growth, or lower required return → higher justified P/E.
Enterprise value
EV = Market cap + Debt + Preferred − Cash
EV/EBITDA compares firms with different leverage.
Two-stage dividend discount model
High growth for a few years, then constant growth forever. The chart shows how sensitive value is to the long-term growth rate.

Method of comparables

Compare a stock's multiple with peers' or the industry's. A lower multiple may mean undervalued, or that the company deserves it (slower growth, higher risk). Relative, not absolute, valuation.

Choosing a model

DDM: stable dividend payers. FCFE: firms whose dividends differ from their capacity to pay. Multiples: quick comparisons, and companies without dividends. Asset-based: firms with mostly tangible or financial assets.
