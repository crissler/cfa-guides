Profit maximization

Every firm produces where marginal revenue = marginal cost. Under perfect competition P = MR, so P = MC. A firm with pricing power faces downward-sloping demand, so MR < P and it sets P > MC.

Oligopoly models

Kinked demand (rivals match price cuts, not increases, so prices are sticky); Cournot (each firm takes rivals' output as fixed; outcome between monopoly and competition); Stackelberg (a leader moves first and gains); Nash equilibrium (no one gains by changing strategy alone; collusion is unstable); dominant firm (sets price, others follow).

Economies of scale

Long-run average cost falls as output grows (economies of scale), then rises (diseconomies). The lowest point is the minimum efficient scale. In the long run, competitive firms produce at minimum average total cost.
Shutdown and breakeven
A competitive firm takes the market price. It produces where price = marginal cost, then decides whether to keep operating.

Decision rules

P ≥ ATC: profitable (breakeven at minimum ATC). AVC ≤ P < ATC: operate in the short run (revenue covers variable costs and part of fixed costs), exit in the long run. P < AVC: shut down now.

Measuring concentration

N-firm concentration ratio: sum of the largest N firms' market shares (ignores the market's structure within those firms). Herfindahl–Hirschman index: sum of squared market shares (in %); 10,000 for a monopoly; more sensitive to mergers among large firms. Neither captures barriers to entry or potential competition.

Part 2 · Learning module 2

Understanding business cycles

The curriculum mainly uses the growth cycle: fluctuations of output around its long-run trend (potential output). The classical cycle tracks the level of activity; the growth rate cycle tracks its growth rate and signals turning points earliest.

The four phases
The output gap (actual minus potential output) over one cycle.
PhaseOutput gapWhat happens
RecoveryNegative, starting to narrow (trough)Spending begins to pick up; firms rebuild inventory; hiring lags; inflation still low
ExpansionPositive and widening (boom)Strong growth; firms hire and invest; prices and interest rates may rise; shortages can appear
SlowdownPositive, starting to narrow (peak)Growth slows; firms use overtime rather than new hires; inflation eventually slows
ContractionNegative and wideningConfidence falls; firms cut overtime, then jobs; recession (or depression if severe)

Credit cycles

Credit availability and asset prices (especially property) expand and contract together, amplifying the business cycle. Credit cycles tend to be longer and deeper than business cycles; booms that are financed by credit can end in severe busts.

Leads and lags in decisions

Firms adjust hours and overtime before hiring or firing; capital spending follows orders and capacity use; inventories pile up at peaks (the inventory–sales ratio rises) and are run down in early recovery.

Nowcasting

Estimating the current state of the economy before official data arrive, using high-frequency data, surveys and big data (e.g. the Atlanta Fed's GDPNow).
Indicator typeTurning pointsExamples
LeadingBefore the economyStock prices, house prices, retail sales, interest rate spread (long − short), building permits, consumer expectations, average weekly hours in manufacturing, manufacturers' new orders

Part 3 · Learning module 3

Fiscal policy

Fiscal policy is the government's use of spending and taxation to influence the economy. Its roles: redistributing income and wealth, allocating resources (e.g. public goods), and stabilizing the economy (smoothing the cycle).

Tools

Spending: transfer payments (welfare, pensions), current spending (wages, goods and services), capital spending (infrastructure). Revenue: direct taxes (on income, wealth, profits) and indirect taxes (on spending: VAT, excise duties). A good tax is simple, efficient, fair and raises enough revenue.

Deficits and debt: concerns

Higher future taxes; crowding out (government borrowing pushes up rates and reduces private investment); inflation if the central bank finances deficits; reduced fiscal room in the next crisis.

Deficits and debt: counterpoints

Debt owed to domestic citizens is partly money owed to ourselves; deficits that fund productive investment can raise future output; Ricardian equivalence: if people expect higher future taxes, they save today's tax cut, so it has little effect.
Fiscal multiplier
11 − MPC(1 − t)
MPC = marginal propensity to consume; t = tax rate. The balanced budget multiplier is positive: raising spending and taxes by the same amount still increases output.

Fiscal stance

A deficit alone doesn't show whether policy is expansionary: deficits rise automatically in recessions. The structural (cyclically adjusted) budget balance is what the balance would be at full employment and shows the true stance.

Automatic stabilizers vs discretionary policy

Progressive taxes and unemployment benefits automatically cushion downturns and cool booms. Discretionary policy needs new decisions and suffers lags: recognition (seeing the problem), action (passing the law), impact (the effect arriving).

Other difficulties

Uncertainty about where the economy is; crowding out; supply constraints (more demand just raises prices); unemployment that isn't due to weak demand; political pressure.
Part 4 · Learning module 4

Monetary policy

Roles of a central bank

Monopoly supplier of the currency; banker to the government and to banks; lender of last resort; regulator and supervisor of the payment system and often of banks; manager of FX and gold reserves; conductor of monetary policy.

Objectives

Mainly price stability (low, stable inflation), often through an inflation target; some central banks also target full employment and stable growth, or an exchange rate. Unexpected inflation redistributes wealth and creates uncertainty; even expected inflation causes menu costs and shoe-leather costs.

Tools

Open market operations (buying or selling government bonds); the policy rate (the rate at which it lends to banks); reserve requirements. When rates hit their lower bound: quantitative easing (large asset purchases) and forward guidance.
Policy rate

Central bank changes its official rate.

Market rates

Bank lending and bond yields follow.

Asset prices and expectations

Share and house prices, confidence, and the exchange rate adjust.

Spending

Consumption, investment and net exports change.

Inflation

Demand pressure and inflation respond, with long and variable lags.

Effective central banks are…

Independent (operationally free from political pressure), credible (people believe the target), and transparent (clear communication). Credibility anchors inflation expectations.

Stance

The neutral rate neither speeds up nor slows the economy: roughly trend growth + inflation target. A policy rate above it is contractionary; below it is expansionary.

Limitations

Long-term rates and expectations may not follow; in a liquidity trap people hoard cash and rate cuts don't work; deflation makes real rates too high at the zero bound; QE's effects are uncertain; banks may not lend.

Money: a medium of exchange, store of value and unit of account. Under fractional reserve banking, deposits create more deposits: money multiplier = 1 / reserve requirement. Fisher effect: nominal interest rate ≈ real rate + expected inflation (plus a risk premium for uncertainty).

Part 5 · Learning module 5

Introduction to geopolitics

Actors

State actors: national governments and their institutions. Non-state actors: companies, NGOs, international organizations, individuals and groups that operate across borders.

Political cooperation

Countries cooperate to gain access to resources, set common standards, and build soft power (influence through culture and values). Cooperation depends on the hierarchy of national interests, its costs, the power of the decision maker, and institutions (e.g. WTO, IMF, World Bank).

Globalization

Cross-border flows of goods, capital, people and ideas. Motivations: profit, access to markets and resources, and intrinsic gains (knowledge, culture). Costs: unequal benefits, job losses, dependence on others; risks of rollback and nationalism.

Tools of geopolitics

National security tools (armed conflict, alliances, espionage); economic tools (trade agreements, or sanctions, tariffs and nationalizations); financial tools (open currency exchange and foreign investment, or capital controls and investment restrictions).

Types of geopolitical risk

Event risk: known dates (elections, Brexit). Exogenous risk: sudden and unexpected (invasions, civil unrest). Thematic risk: known, long-running issues (climate change, cyber threats, terrorism).

Assessing and acting

Judge each risk by likelihood, velocity (speed of impact), and the size and nature of its impact. Use scenario analysis and signposts (events that show which scenario is unfolding) rather than single forecasts.
Part 6 · Learning module 6

International trade

Benefits and costs

Benefits: gains from specialization and exchange, economies of scale, more variety, more competition and efficiency, technology transfer. Costs: job and income losses in industries that compete with imports; benefits spread unevenly.

Comparative advantage

A country has an absolute advantage if it produces a good with fewer resources, and a comparative advantage if its opportunity cost is lower. Trade is beneficial whenever opportunity costs differ, even if one country is better at everything.
RestrictionHow it worksMain effects (importing country)
TariffTax on importsHigher domestic price; less consumption and imports; more domestic production; government revenue; deadweight loss
QuotaLimit on import quantityLike a tariff, but the "quota rent" goes to licence holders or foreign exporters instead of the government
Export subsidyPayment to exportersRaises the domestic price in the exporting country; costs taxpayers; can provoke retaliation
Voluntary export restraintExporter agrees to limit exportsLike a quota; the rent goes to foreign exporters

A large country's tariff can lower the world price (a terms-of-trade gain), but restrictions usually reduce total welfare.

Part 7 · Learning module 7

Capital flows and the FX market

Market participants

Sell side: dealers, mostly large banks. Buy side: corporations, real money investors (pension funds, insurers), leveraged accounts (hedge funds), retail investors, governments, central banks and sovereign wealth funds.

Products

Spot (settles in about two days), forwards (any later date), FX swaps (spot plus an offsetting forward), futures and options.

Quotes

"USD/EUR = 1.10" means 1.10 US dollars per one euro: EUR is the base currency, USD the price currency. A direct quote is domestic currency per unit of foreign currency; an indirect quote is the reverse.
Real exchange rate
Reald/f = Sd/f × PfPd
Adjusts the nominal rate for relative price levels: the cost of foreign goods in terms of domestic goods.

Trade balance and capital flows

A country with a trade deficit must finance it with capital inflows (borrowing from or selling assets to foreigners); a surplus country lends abroad. Exchange rates move with both trade and capital flows.

Why restrict capital flows

Reduce exchange rate volatility and "hot money"; keep an independent monetary policy alongside a fixed exchange rate; protect strategic industries or domestic savers; preserve reserves in a crisis.

Forms and costs

Taxes on flows, limits on foreign ownership, minimum holding periods, outright bans. They can reduce efficiency, raise the cost of capital and invite evasion.

Exchange rate calculations

Cross rate
A/C = A/B × B/C
Line up the currencies so the middle one cancels; invert a quote if needed (B/A = 1 ÷ A/B).
Forward rate (covered interest parity)
Fp/b = Sp/b × 1 + rp(Actual/360)1 + rb(Actual/360)
p = price currency, b = base currency.
Forward points
(F − S) × 10,000
For quotes to 4 decimals. Positive points = the base currency trades at a forward premium.
Rule of thumb. The currency with the lower interest rate trades at a forward premium; the higher-rate currency trades at a forward discount. Otherwise you could earn the higher rate risk-free.
