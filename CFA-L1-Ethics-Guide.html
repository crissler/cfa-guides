Exam game plan

What candidates who passed wish they'd known going in.

Why ethics matters so much

  • It's one of the largest topics at Level I, and the same Standards are tested at all three levels. Learn them properly once.
  • CFA Institute has historically said it may use ethics performance to decide borderline pass/fail results, so strong ethics can tip a close score.
  • Questions are short scenarios: identify the standard involved, then decide whether there's a violation and what the person should do.
  • The exam questions are modeled on the examples in the Standards of Practice Handbook (free on the CFA Institute site). Reading it is the single best ethics study step.

Highest-yield items, ranked

  1. II(A) Material nonpublic information and the mosaic theory
  2. I(B) Gifts and independence vs IV(B) written consent
  3. III(B) Fair dealing: trade and IPO allocation
  4. I(A) Strictest law, dissociation
  5. VII(B) How to refer to the CFA designation
  6. IV(A) Leaving an employer; IV(C) supervisors
  7. III(A) Soft dollars, proxy voting, who the client is
  8. III(C) Suitability and unsolicited trades
  9. VI(B) Priority of transactions; VI(C) referral fees
  10. GIPS: who can claim compliance, composites, verification
How to answer ethics questions. (1) Ask who is owed a duty: clients first, then employer, then yourself. (2) Find the standard the scenario is testing. (3) Check whether the action required is disclosure, written consent, or refusal (see the cheat sheet). (4) Watch the wording: "most likely violated", "least likely", "best action". A "no violation" choice is often the right answer when the person disclosed properly or followed policy.
Top traps. Thinking local law is enough when the Code is stricter. Treating disclosure as enough when written consent is required (IV(B)). Thinking "fair" means "equal" (III(B)). Assuming any information from a company insider is MNPI (it must be material and nonpublic). Writing "CFA Level II" or "a CFA". Assuming individuals can claim GIPS compliance.
Part 1 · Learning module 1

Ethics and trust in the investment profession

Why ethics matters, what makes a profession, and a framework for making decisions.

Why trust matters

Clients hand over money and rely on advice they can't easily check. Without trust, they invest less, demand more protection, and markets work worse. Ethical conduct builds trust, which benefits clients, firms, the profession and society.

What makes a profession

  • Specialized knowledge and skills
  • High entry standards (e.g. exams, experience)
  • A code of ethics and standards of conduct
  • Monitoring and enforcement of conduct
  • A professional body that develops members and serves society

1.1 Ethics vs law

Legal isn't the same as ethical
Laws set a minimum and often lag behind new practices. Ethics often asks for more.

1.2 The ethical decision-making framework

1 · Identify

Relevant facts, stakeholders and the duties you owe them, ethical principles, and conflicts of interest.

2 · Consider

Situational influences, additional guidance (compliance, mentors, the Code), and alternative actions.

3 · Decide and act

Choose and take the action that best fulfills your duties.

4 · Reflect

Was the outcome what you expected? What would you do differently next time?

What gets in the way

Overconfidence ("I'm an ethical person, so this must be fine"). Situational influences: money, prestige, loyalty to a boss or colleagues, peer pressure. Short-term focus: bonuses and quarterly targets crowd out long-term consequences.

Ethical vs unethical decisions

Ethical decisions consider the impact on all stakeholders, not just yourself or your firm. A useful test: would you be comfortable if the decision were reported on the front page of a newspaper?
Part 2 · Learning module 2

The Code of Ethics, Standards and Professional Conduct Program

The six principles of the Code, the map of the seven Standards, and how violations are handled.

2.1 The Code of Ethics (paraphrased)

1 · Integrity. Act with integrity, competence, diligence and respect, and ethically, toward the public, clients, prospects, employers, employees, colleagues and other market participants.
2 · Clients first. Put the integrity of the profession and clients' interests above your own.
3 · Care and judgment. Use reasonable care and independent professional judgment in analysis, recommendations and actions.
4 · Professional conduct. Practice, and encourage others to practice, in a professional and ethical way that reflects well on you and the profession.
5 · Market integrity. Promote the integrity and viability of global capital markets for the benefit of society.
6 · Competence. Maintain and improve your own competence, and strive to improve that of other investment professionals.

2.2 Map of the Standards

Click any standard to jump to its summary.

2.3 Priority of duties

1 · Clients (and the integrity of markets)
2 · Employer
3 · You

When duties conflict, serve them in this order. Loyalty to your employer never requires harming clients or breaking the law.

2.4 Professional Conduct Program

1 · Trigger

Self-disclosure on the annual Professional Conduct Statement, written complaints, public information (news, regulators), exam proctor reports, or analysis of exam results and materials.

2 · Investigation

Professional Conduct staff investigate. They may ask for documents and an explanation; you must cooperate.

3 · Outcome

No action, a cautionary letter, further investigation, or a proposed sanction.

4 · Hearing

If you accept the sanction, it's imposed. If you reject it, a hearing panel of the Disciplinary Review Committee decides.

Sanctions for members

Public censure, suspension of membership and of the right to use the CFA designation, or revocation.

Sanctions for candidates

Suspension from, or a permanent ban on, taking CFA exams; exam results can be voided.

Members sign the Professional Conduct Statement every year; candidates sign it when they register for an exam.

Part 7 · Learning modules 4–5

Global Investment Performance Standards (GIPS) and ethics application

Why GIPS exist, who can claim compliance, composites and verification.

Why GIPS exist

Voluntary, global standards created by CFA Institute so that investment performance is presented with fair representation and full disclosure. They stop practices like showing only the best accounts or the best time periods, and they let investors compare firms on a like-for-like basis across countries.

Who can claim compliance

  • Firms (and asset owners), never individuals, software, or a single product
  • Compliance is firm-wide: the firm must meet all applicable requirements
  • No partial claims ("complies except for…")
  • Must use the required compliance statement wording

7.1 Composites

Every portfolio goes into a composite
A composite groups portfolios managed to a similar strategy or mandate. All actual, fee-paying, discretionary portfolios must be in at least one composite, so the firm can't hide its losers.

Only allowed when the portfolio isn't fee-paying or discretionary (e.g. client restrictions stop the firm from applying its strategy).

Performance history

A firm must first present at least 5 years of GIPS-compliant performance (or since inception if shorter), then add a year each year until it shows at least 10 years.

Verification

An optional review by an independent third party. It covers the whole firm: whether composites are built correctly and whether policies and calculation procedures meet GIPS. It isn't done composite by composite, and it isn't required to claim compliance, but it adds credibility.

Benefits for firms

Credibility with prospects, ability to compete globally, and better internal controls and data.

Benefits for investors

Confidence that performance is complete and fairly presented, and easier comparison between managers.
GIPS vs Standard III(D). III(D) requires fair, accurate and complete performance presentation. Complying with GIPS is one way to meet it, but the Standards don't require GIPS compliance.

7.2 Ethics application

The final module is case studies that combine several standards at once. Practice spotting every standard a scenario touches: one action often violates two (e.g. trading ahead of clients on inside information breaks II(A) and VI(B)).

Practice the core skill

Which standard is it?

Test yourself

Practice questions

Work each one before opening the answer.

1An analyst works in Country A, whose laws are less strict than the Code. Her clients are in Country B, whose laws are stricter than the Code and apply to this business. She should follow: A. Country A law B. the Code and Standards C. Country B lawShow answer
C. Follow the strictest applicable rule. Country B's law applies and is stricter than the Code. Standard I(A).
2A portfolio manager learns that her firm is front-running client orders. Her most appropriate action is to: A. report the firm to regulators immediately B. dissociate from the activity, for example by reporting it to compliance and refusing to take part C. do nothing, because she isn't personally involvedShow answer
B. The Standards require dissociation. Reporting to regulators isn't required by the Standards (though it may be by law). Doing nothing can count as participation. Standard I(A).
3A grateful client gives an adviser concert tickets for last year's strong returns. The adviser: A. must refuse B. may accept, and should disclose it to her employer C. needs written consent from her employer firstShow answer
B. A thank-you for past performance poses little threat to independence. Disclose to the employer. C would apply if the gift were tied to future performance (IV(B)). Standard I(B).
4A broker offers a fund manager a luxury golf weekend, hoping to win more of the fund's trades. The manager should: A. accept and disclose it B. accept if her employer consents C. refuseShow answer
C. A lavish gift from someone seeking to influence your decisions could reasonably compromise your independence. Standard I(B).
5A company pays an analyst to write a research report on it. The arrangement most consistent with the Standards is: A. a flat fee agreed in advance, disclosed in the report B. a fee that rises if the stock price rises C. payment in the company's sharesShow answer
A. Pay must not depend on the conclusions or the stock's performance, and the arrangement must be disclosed. Standard I(B).
6Which is least likely a violation of Standard I(C) Misrepresentation? A. Using a chart from a competitor's report without citing it B. Using index return data from a recognized financial data service without citing it C. Telling clients a corporate bond fund "can't lose money"Show answer
B. Factual data from recognized financial and statistical reporting services can be used without acknowledgment. A is plagiarism; C guarantees performance of a risky investment.
7A charterholder files for personal bankruptcy after losing his job. No fraud was involved. This is: A. a violation of I(D) Misconduct B. not a violation C. a violation of I(E) CompetenceShow answer
B. Personal financial trouble without dishonesty or fraud doesn't reflect on professional integrity. Standard I(D).
8At a restaurant, an analyst overhears a CEO say his company will announce a big acquisition next week. The analyst: A. may trade, since she got it by accident B. may tell clients but not trade herself C. must not trade or cause others to tradeShow answer
C. It's material and nonpublic regardless of how she learned it. Standard II(A).
9An analyst combines public filings with nonmaterial observations from store visits and concludes a retailer will miss earnings. Recommending a sale is: A. a violation of II(A) B. allowed under the mosaic theory C. allowed only after the earnings releaseShow answer
B. Combining public information with nonmaterial nonpublic information is allowed, even if the conclusion would be material on its own.
10A trader repeatedly buys and sells a thinly traded stock between accounts he controls to make it look actively traded. This violates: A. II(A) B. II(B) C. III(B)Show answer
B. Transaction-based manipulation: creating a false impression of volume to mislead the market.
11For a company's defined benefit pension plan, the manager's duty of loyalty is owed to: A. the company's management B. the plan's participants and beneficiaries C. the plan's trustees onlyShow answer
B. The "client" is the plan's participants and beneficiaries. Standard III(A).
12Which use of client brokerage commissions (soft dollars) is acceptable? A. Research that benefits the clients whose trades paid for it B. The firm's office rent C. Marketing materialsShow answer
A. Brokerage belongs to the client and may only buy research and services that benefit the client. Standard III(A).
13A hot IPO is oversubscribed. The best allocation is: A. to the firm's largest clients first B. pro rata to all clients for whom it's suitable, before any personal or family accounts C. equally to every client, suitable or notShow answer
B. Fair dealing and priority of transactions. Standards III(B) and VI(B).
14A firm offers a premium service with faster research delivery for a higher fee. Under fair dealing this is: A. a violation, because all clients must be treated equally B. acceptable if disclosed and available to all clients C. acceptable only for institutional clientsShow answer
B. Fair doesn't mean equal. Different service levels are fine if disclosed and offered to everyone, and they don't disadvantage other clients. Standard III(B).
15A conservative client insists on a large speculative purchase that would materially change his portfolio's risk. The adviser should: A. execute it without comment B. refuse to execute it C. discuss its unsuitability; if the client insists, update the IPS or reconsider the relationshipShow answer
C. Standard III(C) Suitability, for unsolicited trades.
16A manager presents performance for her five best accounts and omits closed accounts. This violates: A. III(D) B. V(B) C. I(D)Show answer
A. Performance presentation must be fair, accurate and complete.
17An adviser gives client records to CFA Institute's Professional Conduct staff during an investigation. This: A. violates III(E) B. doesn't violate III(E) C. requires the client's written permission firstShow answer
B. Cooperating with a Professional Conduct Program investigation isn't a breach of confidentiality.
18Before resigning to start her own firm, an analyst emails her employer's clients inviting them to follow her. This: A. violates IV(A) B. is fine if she has no non-compete agreement C. is fine if she uses her personal emailShow answer
A. Soliciting clients before leaving breaches the duty of loyalty. After she leaves, she may contact them using public information or memory, unless a non-compete applies.
19A client offers his portfolio manager a bonus if the portfolio beats its benchmark next year. The manager must: A. refuse B. disclose it to clients C. get written consent from her employer before acceptingShow answer
C. Compensation tied to future performance could create a conflict with the employer's interests. Standard IV(B).
20A new head of compliance finds the firm's compliance procedures inadequate. She should: A. accept the role and fix things over time B. decline in writing to accept supervisory responsibility until adequate procedures are adopted C. resignShow answer
B. Standard IV(C) Responsibilities of Supervisors.
21An analyst disagrees with her team's buy recommendation, which was reached through a sound, well-researched process. She: A. must remove her name from the report B. may keep her name on it and document her dissent C. must publish her own opposing reportShow answer
B. The test is whether the group's recommendation has a reasonable and adequate basis. Standard V(A).
22With no regulatory requirement in place, CFA Institute recommends keeping records for at least: A. 3 years B. 5 years C. 7 yearsShow answer
C. Standard V(C) Record Retention.
23A manager's son has a fee-paying account at the firm. When the manager makes a block purchase, he excludes his son's account to avoid any appearance of favoritism. This: A. is required by VI(B) B. disadvantages a client and is inconsistent with the Standards C. is a best practiceShow answer
B. Family members' client accounts should be treated like any other client account, neither favored nor disadvantaged.
24An adviser receives a fee from an accountant for each client referred, and tells only his employer. This: A. complies with VI(C) B. violates VI(C) because clients and prospects must also be told C. violates IV(B) onlyShow answer
B. Referral arrangements must be disclosed to employer, clients and prospects.
25After the exam, a candidate posts that "there were a lot of questions on duration and convexity." This: A. is fine, since it's general B. violates VII(A) C. violates VII(B)Show answer
B. Revealing which topics or formulas were tested breaches exam confidentiality. Saying the exam was hard is fine.
26Which is an acceptable reference? A. "Jane Lee, CFA Level II" B. "Jane Lee, Level II candidate in the CFA Program" C. "Jane Lee, CFA (expected 2027)"Show answer
B. There's no partial designation. Standard VII(B).
27Who can claim compliance with GIPS? A. An individual portfolio manager B. An investment management firm, for the whole firm C. A firm, for selected composites onlyShow answer
B. Compliance is firm-wide; individuals and partial claims aren't allowed.
28GIPS verification is: A. required and done composite by composite B. optional and done for the firm as a whole C. required every yearShow answer
B. Verification is recommended but voluntary, and covers the entire firm.
