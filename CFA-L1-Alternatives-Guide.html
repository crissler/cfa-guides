Part 1 · Learning module 1

Features, methods and structures

What counts as "alternative"

Anything other than traditional public equity, bonds and cash. Categories: private capital (private equity, private debt), real assets (real estate, infrastructure, natural resources), hedge funds, and increasingly digital assets.

Typical characteristics

Illiquid; less transparent; specialized knowledge needed; high fees; often leverage; returns not normally distributed; low correlation with traditional assets; long time horizons; complex legal and tax structures; lighter regulation.

Why invest

Diversification, potential for higher returns (including an illiquidity premium), access to strategies and assets not available in public markets, and inflation protection for some real assets.

1.2 Ownership and compensation structures

Limited partnership

The general partner (GP) manages the fund and has unlimited liability. Limited partners (LPs) are passive investors whose liability is capped at their investment. Terms are set in the limited partnership agreement (LPA); side letters can give some LPs special terms. Other forms: joint ventures, separately managed accounts.

Fees

Management fee: usually 1–2% of committed capital (private equity) or of assets under management (hedge funds). Performance fee (incentive fee, carried interest): typically 20% of profits, often only above a hurdle rate.

Waterfalls

Deal-by-deal (American): GP earns carry on each successful deal as it exits; favors the GP. Whole-of-fund (European): LPs get back all contributed capital plus the hurdle first; favors LPs.

Protecting LPs

High-water mark: no performance fee until the fund's value exceeds its previous peak (net of fees), so LPs don't pay twice for the same gains. Clawback: LPs can reclaim performance fees already paid if later losses mean the GP was overpaid.

Other arrangements

Founders shares: lower fees (e.g. 1.5% and 10%) for early investors. Either/or fees: the manager earns either a low management fee (e.g. 1%) or a higher performance fee (e.g. 30% above a hurdle), whichever is greater. Lockups and redemption notice periods limit withdrawals.

Performance appraisal and returns

Investment life cycle

Capital commitment (fees start, little income) → capital deployment (cash calls, investing) → capital distribution (exits, cash returned). Returns are typically negative early and positive later: the J-curve.

Why appraisal is hard

Long horizons, irregular cash flows, leverage, illiquidity, complex fees, appraisal-based (smoothed) valuations, and non-normal returns. Returns depend on when an investor came in and on their specific fee terms.

Part 3 · Learning module 3

Private capital: equity and debt

Private equity categoryWhat it does
Leveraged buyout (LBO)Buys a mature company with lots of debt. Management buyout (MBO): existing managers buy; management buy-in (MBI): outside managers come in.
Venture capitalFunds young companies by stage: pre-seed/angel → seed → early stage (Series A…) → later stage → mezzanine (pre-IPO). Highest risk; most fail, a few big winners.
Growth equityMinority stakes in established, growing companies needing capital to expand; little or no leverage.

Exit strategies

Trade sale to a strategic buyer (most common) · IPO (highest valuations but costly and slow) · SPAC merger · recapitalization (re-lever and pay a dividend) · secondary sale to another PE fund · write-off/liquidation.

How PE creates value

Operational improvements; better governance and aligned incentives; financial engineering (leverage); buying low and selling at a higher multiple.
Private debtFeatures
Direct lendingSenior, secured loans from non-bank lenders, with covenants; the largest category.
Mezzanine debtSubordinated debt, often with warrants or conversion features; higher return than senior debt.
Venture debtLoans to early-stage firms with little cash flow, often with equity warrants.
Distressed debtBuying debt of companies near or in bankruptcy, aiming to profit from restructuring.
Unitranche and specialtyUnitranche blends senior and subordinated into one loan at one rate; specialty lending covers niches (e.g. litigation finance, royalties).

Risk and return: private equity (especially VC) has higher expected returns and risk than private debt; private debt earns more than public bonds because of illiquidity and complexity. Both offer some diversification, though smoothed valuations overstate it.

Part 4 · Learning module 4

Real estate and infrastructure

Real estate sectors

Residential (homes, apartments): the largest sector, about 75% of the global market; cash flow from rents or mortgage payments by households. Commercial: offices, retail, industrial and warehouses, hotels; cash flow from business tenants' leases.

Unique features

Heterogeneity (no two properties are alike), indivisibility, fixed location, fragmented markets, hard price discovery, high and slow transaction costs, illiquidity.
EquityDebt
PrivateDirect ownership; private real estate fundsMortgages; private real estate debt funds
PublicREITs (real estate investment trusts) and real estate company sharesMortgage-backed securities

Investment characteristics: returns from rental income plus appreciation; partial inflation hedge; diversification; risks from leverage, interest rates, vacancies, tenant credit and location. REITs offer liquidity but behave more like equities in the short run.

4.1 Infrastructure

Types

Economic: transportation (roads, airports, ports), utilities, energy, communications. Social: schools, hospitals, social housing, correctional facilities.

Stage of development

Greenfield: new, to be built (higher risk; often build-operate-transfer in public–private partnerships). Secondary stage. Brownfield: existing, operating assets with track records (lower risk).

Cash flow sources

Availability payments (paid to keep the facility available), usage-based payments (tolls, fees), and take-or-pay contracts (buyers pay for a minimum volume).

Characteristics: long-lived assets, often monopoly-like with high barriers to entry, stable and inflation-linked cash flows, low correlation with other assets. Risks: construction, regulatory and political, demand, and leverage. Increasingly financed privately through public–private partnerships.

Part 5 · Learning module 5

Natural resources

Raw land

Undeveloped land; returns depend on future development or use; no income meanwhile.

Timberland

Returns from biological growth, timber prices and land value. Owners can delay harvest when prices are low (the trees keep growing), which adds flexibility.

Farmland

Returns from crop income and land value. Crops must be harvested when ready, so there's less flexibility than timber. Exposed to weather and crop prices.

5.1 Commodities

How investors get exposure

Mostly through futures (and funds that hold them), plus commodity company shares. Commodity prices depend on supply and demand; supply adjusts slowly (mines, crops), so prices can be volatile.

Forward curve shapes

Contango: forward prices above spot (cost of carry exceeds the convenience yield); hurts long-only investors who roll futures. Backwardation: forward prices below spot (high convenience yield, the benefit of holding the physical commodity); helps long investors.
Contango vs backwardation
Forward prices by contract maturity, with the spot price at 100.

Role in a portfolio: natural resources can hedge inflation and diversify equity and bond risk; farmland and timberland returns are smoothed by appraisals; commodities are volatile and earn no income except through the futures structure.

Part 6 · Learning module 6

Hedge funds

Private, pooled investment vehicles for institutions and wealthy investors, with flexible strategies: leverage, short selling, derivatives, and concentrated positions. Lightly regulated; high minimums; lockups, notice periods and gates limit redemptions.

Strategy groupExamples
EquityLong/short equity; equity market neutral; short-biased; fundamental or quantitative stock picking
Event-drivenMerger arbitrage (buy the target, short the acquirer); distressed/restructuring; activist; special situations
Relative valueFixed-income arbitrage; convertible bond arbitrage (long convertible, short stock); volatility trading
OpportunisticGlobal macro (bets on rates, currencies, commodities based on economic views); managed futures / CTAs (often trend following)
Multi-managerFund of funds; multi-strategy funds

Master–feeder structure

Onshore and offshore feeder funds both invest in one master fund that holds the portfolio. Set up for tax efficiency for different investor types.

Separately managed account

The investor owns the assets directly; full transparency and customization; higher minimums and costs for the manager.

Fund of funds

Diversification across managers and access to closed funds, plus due diligence, but an extra layer of fees.

Prime brokers provide leverage (margin financing), securities lending for short sales, custody, clearing and reporting. Risk and return: returns depend on manager skill and strategy; leverage and illiquidity add risk; reported indexes suffer from survivorship and backfill bias; diversification benefits fall in crises when correlations rise.

Part 7 · Learning module 7

Introduction to digital assets

Distributed ledger technology

A shared, synchronized database across many participants (nodes). A blockchain bundles transactions into blocks chained together cryptographically, creating an immutable history. Can make post-trade processing, compliance and identity checks more efficient using smart contracts (self-executing code).

Networks and consensus

Permissionless networks are open to anyone (e.g. Bitcoin); permissioned networks restrict who can participate. The consensus protocol sets the rules for adding blocks: proof of work (miners solve puzzles; energy-intensive) or proof of stake (validators stake coins).

How they differ from traditional assets

Usually no underlying cash flows or fundamental value; validated by a network instead of an intermediary; mostly not legal tender; weaker legal and regulatory protection.
TypeWhat it is
CryptocurrenciesBitcoin, plus altcoins: stablecoins (pegged to a fiat currency or other assets) and meme coins. Often a capped supply.
Non-fungible tokens (NFTs)Unique tokens representing ownership of a specific digital or physical item
Security tokensDigital form of a security (ownership, debt, revenue rights)
Utility tokensAccess to a product or service on a platform
Governance tokensVoting rights over a protocol's rules
Asset-backed tokensDigital claims on physical or financial assets that collateralize them

How to invest

Direct: buy coins or tokens (held in wallets), on centralized or decentralized exchanges, or in initial coin offerings. Indirect: crypto futures (e.g. on regulated exchanges), exchange-traded products, trusts, hedge funds, and shares of crypto-related companies. Exchanges of both types face fraud and manipulation risk and limited oversight.

Risk, return and diversification

Prices are driven by expectations and demand for a limited supply, not by cash flows. Bitcoin has shown high returns, high volatility and low correlation with traditional assets, though correlations have risen in stressed markets. DeFi (decentralized finance) builds financial services from open-source smart contracts.
