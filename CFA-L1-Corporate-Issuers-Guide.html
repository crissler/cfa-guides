  • Mostly concepts and comparisons: organizational forms, debt vs equity holders, governance mechanisms, working capital approaches, capital structure theories, business model types.
  • A handful of calculations: cash conversion cycle, cost of trade credit, NPV, IRR, ROIC, WACC, and the Modigliani–Miller formulas.

  7. Organizational forms; public vs private companies
  8. Static trade-off and pecking order theories

Top traps. Forgetting the (1 − t) on the cost of debt in WACC. Using book weights when market weights are given. Treating sunk costs or financing costs as project cash flows. Corporations dominate because limited liability and transferable ownership make it easy to raise large amounts of capital from many investors. 1.1 Public vs private companies

Public companies

Shares trade on an exchange; extensive, regular disclosure; liquid shares and easier access to capital; more scrutiny and reporting costs; shareholder base is wide and changes daily.

Private companies

Shares aren't listed; limited disclosure; illiquid ownership; owners often involved in management; funding from private equity, venture capital and loans.

Going public

IPO (underwritten offering of new shares), direct listing (existing shares listed, no new capital raised, no underwriter), or merging with a SPAC (a listed shell company that raised cash to buy a private company).

Going private

A buyer (often private equity, in a leveraged buyout, or management, in a management buyout) acquires all the public shares and delists the company.

Share counts

Authorized = maximum the company may issue; issued = sold to date; outstanding = issued minus treasury shares (bought back).
Part 2 · Learning module 2

Investors and other stakeholders

Debtholders

Contractual claim to interest and principal; priority over shareholders; upside capped at the promised payments; prefer lower risk and more stable cash flows; protected by covenants and collateral.

Shareholders

Residual claim after everyone else; no promised payments; unlimited upside, loss limited to the investment; voting rights; benefit from higher risk (it raises the value of their "option" on the firm).
Payoffs to debt and equity at maturity
Debt promises a fixed face value. Equity gets whatever is left. Equity's payoff has the shape of a call option on the firm's assets.
Debtholders: min(V, D)Shareholders: max(0, V − D)
Debtholders get
Shareholders get
The core conflict. Shareholders gain from risky projects (they keep the upside; debtholders share the downside), from paying large dividends, and from adding debt. Debtholders want the opposite. Covenants and collateral exist to limit this.

Part 3 · Learning module 3

Corporate governance: conflicts, mechanisms, risks and benefits

A principal–agent relationship exists when one party (the principal) hires another (the agent) to act on its behalf. Conflicts arise because the agent's interests differ and the principal can't fully observe what the agent does (information asymmetry).

Shareholders vs managers

Excessive pay and perquisites; empire building; too little risk-taking (protecting their jobs) or too much (chasing bonuses); entrenchment; withholding information.

Controlling vs minority shareholders

A controlling owner can push decisions that benefit them at minority holders' expense (related-party deals, board seats). Dual-class share structures give some shares more votes than their economic stake.

Shareholders vs creditors

Riskier investments, extra borrowing, or large dividends and buybacks that shift value from creditors to shareholders.

Other stakeholders

Cost-cutting on product safety (customers), on wages or safety (employees), or on taxes and compliance (government).

Risks of poor governance

Weak control systems and fraud; poor decisions; legal, regulatory and reputational risk; higher default and bankruptcy risk.

Benefits of good governance

Operational efficiency; better controls; better decision-making; lower default risk; a lower cost of debt and equity; better long-term performance.

4.1 Liquidity

Primary sources

Cash and marketable securities; borrowings (banks, bonds, suppliers' trade credit); cash flow from the business (the main long-term source).

Secondary sources

Suspending or cutting dividends; delaying or reducing capital spending; issuing equity; renegotiating debt; selling assets; filing for bankruptcy protection. Using them usually signals financial stress.

Drags and pulls

Drags slow cash inflows: uncollected receivables, obsolete inventory, tighter borrowing conditions. Pulls speed cash outflows: paying suppliers early, reduced credit lines.

Liquidity ratios: current = CA/CL; quick = (cash + marketable securities + receivables)/CL; cash = (cash + marketable securities)/CL.

Types of capital investment

Going concern projects (maintain the business, replace equipment) · regulatory/compliance projects (often required, may not earn a return) · expansion projects (growth; riskier) · other (high-risk, unrelated ventures).

5.1 Principles and pitfalls

Principles

  • Decisions are based on cash flows, not accounting income
  • Cash flows are based on opportunity costs (what you give up)
  • Timing of cash flows matters
  • Cash flows are analyzed after tax
  • Financing costs are excluded: they're in the discount rate
  • Ignore sunk costs; include externalities such as cannibalization

Common pitfalls

  • Inertia: last year's budget drives this year's
  • Source-of-capital bias (spending internal cash more freely)
  • Failing to consider alternatives or improvements
  • Pet projects favored by senior managers
  • Basing decisions on EPS, net income or ROE effects
  • Internal forecasting errors (over-optimism)

5.2 Real options

Timing options: delay the investment until more is known.
Sizing options: abandonment (stop if things go badly) and growth/expansion (invest more if things go well).
Flexibility options: price-setting (raise prices instead of capacity) and production-flexibility (switch inputs, add shifts).
Fundamental options: the project's value depends on an underlying price (e.g. a mine that only pays if the metal price is high).
With an option. Project NPV = NPV without the option − cost of the option + value of the option. A project with a negative NPV can be worth doing if the option is valuable enough.
Part 6 · Learning module 6

Capital structure

Weighted-average cost of capital
WACC = wd rd (1 − t) + wp rp + we re
Use market-value weights (or a target capital structure). Interest is tax-deductible, so debt is cheaper after tax.

Internal factors

Business model and stability of cash flows; asset type (tangible assets support more debt); existing leverage; corporate tax rate; capital structure policies and ratings targets.

External factors

Market conditions and the business cycle; regulation; industry norms.

Company life cycle

Start-up: mostly equity (negative cash flow, few assets). Growth: mostly equity, some secured debt. Mature: more debt (stable cash flows, tangible assets).

6.1 Modigliani–Miller propositions

No taxesWith taxes
Proposition I (firm value)VL = VU: capital structure is irrelevantVL = VU + tD: the interest tax shield adds value
Proposition II (cost of equity)re = r0 + (r0 − rd)(D/E): re rises with leverage; WACC unchangedre = r0 + (r0 − rd)(1 − t)(D/E): WACC falls as debt rises

Assumptions: perfect markets (no taxes, transaction costs or bankruptcy costs), homogeneous expectations, investors can borrow at the risk-free rate, no agency costs, and investment decisions don't depend on financing. r0 = cost of capital of an all-equity firm.

Static trade-off: the tax shield vs the cost of financial distress
Firm value as debt rises. MM without taxes is flat; with taxes it keeps rising; adding financial distress costs gives a peak: the optimal capital structure.
MM, no taxesMM with taxes: VU + tDTrade-off: VU + tD − PV(distress)

Maximum firm value (VU = 100)

Static trade-off theory

VL = VU + tD − PV(costs of financial distress). The optimal capital structure balances the tax benefit against expected distress costs; it minimizes WACC and maximizes firm value.

Pecking order theory

Because managers know more than investors (information asymmetry), firms prefer internal funds first, then debt, then new equity last. Issuing equity can signal that managers think the shares are overvalued.

Agency costs

Debt can discipline managers (less free cash flow to waste). A target capital structure is the mix management aims for over time; actual weights drift around it.
Part 7 · Learning module 7

Business models

Four questions

Who are the customers? What does the firm offer (and why do customers buy it)? Where does it reach customers (channels)? How much does it charge (pricing)?

Value proposition and value chain

The value proposition is why customers choose this product over competitors'. The value chain is how the firm organizes its activities to create that value.

Analyst's job

Build your own understanding of the business model rather than relying on management's description; it drives the forecast and the risk assessment.
Pricing modelHow it works
Value-based vs cost-basedPrice set by value to the customer vs cost plus a markup
Price discrimination, tiered pricing, dynamic pricingDifferent prices for different customers, volumes or times (e.g. airline seats)
Penetration pricingLow initial price to win market share
FreemiumFree basic product, paid premium features
Hidden revenueFree to users; paid by advertisers or data buyers
Recurring revenue / subscriptionRegular payments for ongoing access
Bundling, razors-and-blades, add-onsSell products together; cheap base product with profitable consumables or extras

Conventional models

Manufacturers, distributors and wholesalers, retailers, franchises, service providers. Long established and easy to understand.

Unconventional models

E-commerce and digital platforms, network effects (value grows with users), multi-sided platforms, crowdsourcing, asset-light models, licensing. Often combine or vary conventional models.
